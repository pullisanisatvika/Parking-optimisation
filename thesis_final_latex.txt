\vspace*{0.55in}

\begin{center}
    {\large \bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.35in}

\begin{adjustwidth}{0.45in}{0.45in}


Parking facility search is a major challenge in sustainable and intelligent urban transportation systems. In dense urban road networks, the time spent looking for an available parking space adds to traffic congestion, travel delay, inefficient road usage, higher fuel consumption, and driver frustration. Smart Parking Systems (SPS) have been developed to support parking recommendation through sensing, communication, and intelligent decision-making. However, giving a reliable recommendation in real time is still difficult, because traffic conditions and parking availability can change while the driver is still travelling toward the destination. As a result, a recommendation that is suitable at the start of a route may not remain suitable later in the same route. 

\vspace{0.18in}

Another limitation is that many existing systems perform the recommendation only at the parking lot level. In these approaches, a parking lot is treated as a single available parking option, and the individual parking spots inside the lot are not considered. However, parking lots differ in availability, parking cost, driving time, and walking distance to the destination. Parking spots inside the same lot also differ in availability, spot-specific cost, internal driving time from the entrance, internal walking time to the pedestrian exit, and external walking time to the final destination. Therefore, a complete parking recommendation needs to cover both levels, which include both the parking lot and the individual parking spots within the parking lot. 

\vspace{0.18in}

This thesis addresses the above problem by developing two related frameworks. The first framework focuses on parking lot level recommendation and is referred to as the Dynamic Parking Lot Optimization Framework (DPLOF). DPLOF recomputes the driving route and the recommended parking lot from driver's current location using the latest traffic information available at that point. The framework considers driving time, walking time, and parking cost, together with user-defined constraints such as maximum walking distance and maximum parking cost. The second framework focuses on parking spot level recommendation and is referred to as the Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF). DPSOF continues the decision after a lot has been selected and assigns an individual parking spot inside that lot. In this framework, each available spot is modeled as a separate parking option with its own availability, spot-specific cost, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and proximity to the pedestrian exit.

\vspace{0.18in}

For both frameworks, this thesis develops optimization-based models and evaluates different solution methods for small-, medium-, and large-scale urban road networks. For parking lot level recommendation, the optimization model is first applied to small-scale networks to obtain optimal results and to check whether the formulation works as intended. Since solving the optimization model repeatedly becomes computationally difficult for larger networks, a heuristic method is developed for the medium- and large-scale cases. This heuristic is then compared with meta-heuristic methods such as Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO). For parking spot level recommendation, an optimization model is also formulated, and the same three meta-heuristic methods are applied as scalable comparative methods, so that both problems are studied using a consistent set of solution approaches.

\vspace{0.18in}

The proposed frameworks are evaluated using synthetic urban-like datasets and a real road network case study built in Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO). The synthetic datasets enable controlled variation of network size and experimental parameters, while the SUMO-based network provides a real-world urban road network with directed road links and actual road connectivity. The results for the lot level framework show that the recommendation can be updated from the current vehicle position when traffic conditions change during travel, and that the proposed heuristic gives solution quality close to the optimization model at a much lower computational cost. The spot level framework then narrows the decision further by selecting a suitable parking spot inside the recommended parking lot. 

\vspace{0.18in}

Overall, this thesis presents two dynamic parking recommendation frameworks, one at the parking lot level and the other at the parking spot level. By considering both levels together instead of treating them separately, the proposed work aims to reduce parking facility search time and to support better use of the parking facilities that already exist in urban road networks.
\end{adjustwidth}

\chapter{Introduction}
\begin{thesisbody}

Smart Parking Systems (SPS) are becoming an essential component of intelligent urban transportation networks, particularly in dense urban areas where parking search remains a significant challenge. The rapid growth of urban populations and vehicle ownership has increased parking demand, and this makes it more difficult for drivers to find a suitable parking facility near their destinations. Many drivers spend a considerable amount of time searching for an available parking lot and then for an available spot inside it, contributing to traffic congestion, travel delay, unnecessary vehicle circulation, increased fuel consumption, increased emissions, and reduced road network efficiency \text{\cite{p1-ref1}} \text{\cite{p2-ref1}}. Therefore, improving parking management has become an important goal for supporting urban mobility, reducing the traffic caused by parking facility search, and improving the overall performance of SPS \text{\cite{p1-ref2}}.

\vspace{0.18in}

To address these challenges, SPS have been developed to support parking monitoring, occupancy estimation, availability detection, reservation, pricing, and driver guidance through sensing, communication, and intelligent decision-making technologies \text{\cite{p1-ref3}} \text{\cite{p2-ref2}} \text{\cite{p2-ref3}}. In comparison with traditional parking facility search methods, these systems help drivers identify parking lots and spots more efficiently and reduce the unnecessary effort spent on searching \text{\cite{p1-ref4}}. Existing parking recommendation and allocation approaches commonly consider factors such as travel time, walking distance, parking cost, parking availability, reservation status, and user preferences. Based on these factors, several existing recommendation frameworks have been explored to improve parking recommendations, including multi-objective parking allocation \text{\cite{p2-ref4}}, genetic-algorithm-based parking lot selection \text{\cite{p2-ref5}}, data-driven parking optimization \text{\cite{p2-ref6}}, ant-colony-based dynamic parking recommendation \text{\cite{p2-ref7}}, memetic optimization for street parking allocation \text{\cite{p2-ref8}}, shared parking reservation \text{\cite{p2-ref9}}, and parking allocation under peak demand conditions \text{\cite{p2-ref10}}.

\vspace{0.18in}

Although these advances have improved parking recommendations, providing reliable recommendations in a realistic urban road network remains a challenging problem. Traffic conditions and parking availability can change while a driver is traveling toward a destination, and this makes a recommendation generated at the beginning of a route less suitable later in the same route. Parking facility recommendation therefore depends not only on the availability of a parking lot, but also on the driver's current location, the current traffic condition, the driving time to the parking lot, the internal driving time to the spot, the walking time from the parking spot to the destination, the parking cost of the spot, and user-defined constraints such as maximum walking distance and maximum parking cost. Reservation policies \text{\cite{p1-ref5}}, competition among parking lots \text{\cite{p1-ref6}}, and pricing mechanisms \text{\cite{p1-ref7}} further influence parking access, demand distribution, utilization, and driver selection behavior. Consequently, an effective parking recommendation needs to consider both route-related information and parking-system attributes while recommending a parking facility.

\vspace{0.18in}

Research on SPS has addressed a wide range of parking-management problems, including parking allocation, pricing, assignment, monitoring, reservation, demand estimation, and occupancy inference. Prior studies have improved parking space utilization \text{\cite{p1-ref8}} \text{\cite{p2-ref14}}, studied adaptive pricing under changing demand \text{\cite{p1-ref9}} \text{\cite{p2-ref17}}, optimized travel cost among nearby parking lots \text{\cite{p1-ref10}}, balanced parking demand across multiple parking facilities \text{\cite{p1-ref11}}, and estimated parking occupancy under incomplete sensing infrastructure \text{\cite{p1-ref12}}. Other studies have examined transaction-based occupancy inference \text{\cite{p1-ref13}}, reservation before vehicle arrival \text{\cite{p1-ref14}}, parking space-view-based service improvement \text{\cite{p1-ref15}} \text{\cite{p2-ref28}}, uncertain parking availability \text{\cite{p1-ref16}}, and curb-parking demand influenced by nearby buildings, businesses, and activity areas \text{\cite{p1-ref17}}. Although these studies have contributed significantly to the development of SPS, relatively little attention has been given to parking recommendation methods that continuously update both the routing and the parking decision while a driver is traveling through the road network.

\vspace{0.18in}

Another limitation of many existing parking recommendation systems is that the recommendation is performed mainly at the parking lot level. In these approaches, each parking lot is treated as a single parking option, and the individual parking spots inside the lot are generally not considered during the recommendation process. However, parking spots within the same lot may differ in availability, parking cost, internal driving time from the entrance, walking time to the pedestrian exit, and walking distance to the final destination. Reservation-based valet parking studies have shown that reservation improves parking reliability, but internal movement within the lot remains largely unexplored \text{\cite{p2-ref11}}. Survey studies on smart-city parking and IoT-driven parking management also show that existing systems give priority to sensing, pricing, reservation, and parking lot recommendation rather than to parking spot level routing and assignment \text{\cite{p2-ref12}} \text{\cite{p2-ref13}}. As a result, even after a lot has been recommended, the driver may still need to spend additional time searching inside that lot before finding a suitable available spot.

\vspace{0.18in}

To address these limitations, this thesis treats parking recommendation as a two-level recommendation process consisting of parking lot level recommendation followed by parking spot level recommendation. The first level focuses on dynamic parking lot level recommendation through the proposed Dynamic Parking Lot Optimization Framework (DPLOF). DPLOF updates the traffic information and recomputes both the driving route and the parking lot recommendation from driver's current location as the vehicle moves through the road network. At every node location, the available parking lots are evaluated using an objective function that considers driving time, walking time, parking cost, and user-defined constraints such as maximum walking distance and maximum parking cost. Since the recommendation is updated repeatedly during travel, the framework adapts to changing traffic conditions without relying on future traffic prediction or future parking-price estimation.

\vspace{0.18in}

The second level extends the recommendation from the parking lot level to the parking spot level through the proposed Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF). Instead of treating the parking lot as the final recommendation, DPSOF further evaluates the parking spots available inside the selected lot. For every parking spot, the assignment cost includes external driving time, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and spot-specific parking cost. This allows the recommendation to be made at the parking spot level while remaining fully compatible with the overall graph-based parking recommendation framework.

\vspace{0.18in}

The parking lot level recommendation problem is first formulated as an optimization model for small-scale road networks in order to obtain optimal solutions. As the network size increases, solving the optimization model repeatedly becomes computationally expensive, because the route and the parking recommendation must be updated continuously as traffic conditions change. To address this challenge, a heuristic solution approach is developed for medium- and large-scale road networks. The proposed heuristic is evaluated against three commonly used meta-heuristic methods, namely Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO). GA has been commonly applied to transportation and routing problems \text{\cite{p1-ref18}}. PSO has been used for path planning and search-based optimization \text{\cite{p1-ref19}}, whereas ACO has been applied to congestion-aware route optimization and related transportation problems \text{\cite{p1-ref20}}.

\vspace{0.18in}

For parking spot level recommendation, both an optimization-based recommendation model and the same meta-heuristic methods are considered. The optimization model identifies a suitable parking spot from the set of currently available spots, while GA, PSO, and ACO are used as comparative solution methods based on the proposed spot level cost structure \text{\cite{p2-ref29}}.

\vspace{0.18in}

The proposed frameworks are then evaluated using synthetic urban-like datasets and a real-road network case study developed in Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO). At the lot level, the datasets are varied in network size, edge density, parking supply, traffic level, walking distance threshold, parking cost threshold, and parking cost range. At the spot level, the experiments follow the same dataset variations as in the lot-level analysis, but with the additional refinement that each parking lot is represented by individual parking spots with spot-specific availability, internal walking time, internal driving time, and parking cost. Performance is measured using travel time, walking distance, parking cost, and runtime. The SUMO-based network provides a realistic urban road structure for evaluating the proposed frameworks. The main contributions of this thesis are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}

    \item A dynamic parking recommendation framework is developed both at the parking lot and at the parking spot levels to support real-time parking decisions under changing traffic conditions.

    \item At the parking lot level, DPLOF is proposed to minimize driving time, walking time, and parking cost under user-defined constraints by updating the traffic information and recomputing the route and the parking lot recommendation at each intersection without relying on future conditions.

    \item At the parking spot level, DPSOF is proposed by extending parking recommendation from parking lot level selection to individual parking spot level recommendation.

    \item Each parking spot is modeled as a separate option with its own identifier, availability status, spot-specific cost, internal driving time to the spot, and walking time from the spot to the destination, while internal parking lot movement is represented through weighted links connecting parking lot entrances, parking spots, and pedestrian exits.

    \item Optimization-based models are formulated for both framework levels, and heuristic or meta-heuristic solution approaches are considered for medium- and large-scale instances where repeated optimization becomes computationally expensive.

    \item The parking lot level framework is evaluated using travel time, walking distance, parking cost, and runtime under variations in network size, edge density, parking supply, traffic level, walking distance threshold, parking cost threshold, and parking cost range.

    \item The parking spot level framework is evaluated using external driving time, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, spot-specific parking cost, and runtime under variations in network size, edge density, parking spot availability, number of parking spots per lot, traffic level, walking distance threshold, parking cost threshold, and parking cost range.

    \item SUMO-based real-road network case studies are used to assess both frameworks under realistic urban traffic conditions.

\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.18in}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows:

\begin{itemize}

\item Chapter 2 surveys the relevant literature on smart parking systems, parking recommendation, parking allocation, pricing, reservation, and optimization-based parking recommendation. It also identifies the research gaps and the distinguishing features of the proposed methodologies.

\item Chapter 3 presents the graph creation methodology used throughout this thesis. It describes the generation of synthetic urban-like road networks, the SUMO-based real-road network case study, parking lot and parking spot generation, source and destination placement, and the assignment of travel times, walking distances, and parking costs. This chapter establishes the experimental environment used to evaluate the proposed frameworks under both controlled and realistic urban scenarios.

\item Chapter 4 addresses the first research problem of this thesis, which focuses on real-time parking lot level recommendation. An optimum is formulated for small-scale road networks, while a heuristic solution approach together with GA, PSO, and ACO is considered for medium- and large-scale networks. The framework updates the traffic information and recomputes the route and the parking lot recommendation while considering driving time, walking time, parking cost, and user-defined constraints.

\item Chapter 5 investigates the second research problem of this thesis, which focuses on parking spot level recommendation. An optimization-based recommendation model is first developed for small-scale instances, after which GA, PSO, and ACO are applied to larger problem instances. The framework extends parking recommendation from parking lot selection to individual parking spot recommendation by considering external driving time, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and spot-specific parking cost.

\item Chapter 6 concludes the thesis by summarizing the findings and contributions of this research, discussing its limitations, and outlining future research directions for dynamic parking lot level recommendation and parking spot level recommendation.

\end{itemize}

\end{thesisbody}

\chapter{Related Work}

\begin{thesisbody}
\label{ch:related_work}

 SPS have been studied as a way of reducing the time drivers spend searching for parking and improving the use of limited parking facilities in urban areas. Several survey papers give an overview of this broader research area. Aljohani et al.~\cite{p2-ref1} reviewed parking solutions implemented in smart cities, including parking guidance, sensing, reservation, pricing, and management approaches. Alam et al.~\cite{p2-ref2} explored Internet of Things (IoT)-driven parking management systems and evaluated their sensing technologies, implementation limitations, and future research directions. Agrawal et al.~\cite{p2-ref3} reviewed smart parking techniques intended to improve the use of urban space, while Almalki and Ragab~\cite{p2-ref4} discussed the challenges associated with smart parking in sustainable urban environments. These surveys describe how sensing, communication, automation, and intelligent decision-making have improved parking services in general. However, they remain descriptive in nature. None of them formulates a route-dependent recommendation process nor separates the selection of a parking lot from the selection of a specific spot within that lot. This thesis addresses that gap by formulating parking lot recommendation and parking spot assignment as two connected optimization problems, DPLOF and DPSOF, instead of treating parking management as a single undivided activity.

\vspace{0.18in}

A major direction in SPS research concerns parking occupancy prediction and availability estimation. Li et al.~\cite{p1-ref1} developed a near real-time method for predicting parking occupancy across different parking locations, and their later work used spatial and temporal relationships among nearby parking areas to improve the prediction~\cite{p1-ref2}. Zhao et al.~\cite{p1-ref3} treated parking meters as virtual sensors for estimating citywide on-street parking availability, while Song et al.~\cite{p1-ref4} considered parking vacancy prediction when the sensor information is incomplete. These methods improve the quality and reliability of parking availability information. However, their contribution stops at estimating whether a parking lot is occupied. None of them recomputes the driver's route or re-evaluates a parking lot recommendation as the driver moves and the traffic condition changes. DPLOF addresses this gap by repeatedly recomputing both the route and the parking lot recommendation from driver's current location using the latest available traffic information.

\vspace{0.18in}

Parking monitoring and occupancy inference have also been examined using several data sources. Kim et al.~\cite{p1-ref12} used unmanned aerial vehicles to monitor outdoor parking areas. Assemi et al.~\cite{p1-ref13} inferred on-street parking occupancy from payment transaction records, which provides an alternative when complete sensing infrastructure is unavailable. Nie et al.~\cite{p1-ref17} estimated public curb-parking demand using the distribution of nearby commercial areas, offices, businesses, and public facilities. Abo-Zahhad et al.~\cite{p2-ref16} combined IoT and machine-learning techniques for smart parking management, while He et al.~\cite{p2-ref26} considered occupancy-rate prediction together with customer preferences. In these studies, the resulting information is used as an input to a separate management process rather than as part of a driver-facing recommendation loop. None of these solutions reevaluates the driving route or the parking decision once new information becomes available. In the proposed frameworks, recommendations are recomputed at every intersection using the latest availability and traffic information.

\vspace{0.18in}

Another important research stream focuses on parking pricing, reservation, and competition. Boateng et al.~\cite{p1-ref5} proposed a reservation and dynamic-pricing framework for automated valet parking and charging using multi-agent reinforcement learning. Saharan et al.~\cite{p1-ref6}~\cite{p2-ref13} developed DyPARK, which combines dynamic pricing and allocation for smart on-street parking. Hu et al.~\cite{p1-ref7} examined autonomous valet parking management under demand uncertainty, while Hassija et al.~\cite{p1-ref9} ~\cite{p2-ref27} proposed a parking-slot allocation framework based on virtual voting and adaptive pricing. These studies show that parking demand can be influenced through pricing, reservation, and competition mechanisms. However, their emphasis is on the operation of the parking system rather than on the route-dependent decision made by a driver. No solution evaluates current traffic information, walking time, and parking cost together in the way that a driver approaching a destination has to. In the frameworks, parking cost is instead treated as one of three route-dependent factors, together with driving time and walking time, that are reevaluated at every decision point.

\vspace{0.18in}

Reservation-based parking services have also been used to reduce uncertainty before a vehicle arrives at a parking facility. Hu et al.~\cite{p1-ref14} ~\cite{p1-ref1}~\cite{p2-ref29} proposed CRPark, which combines competition and reservation services in a smart parking system. Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref15}~\cite{p1-ref1}~\cite{p2-ref12} developed PRPSV to improve parking efficiency and reservation services using a parking space view. Zhang et al.~\cite{p2-ref28} studied reservation-enhanced autonomous valet parking under practical operating constraints, while Lian~\cite{p2-ref9} proposed a shared parking spot reservation and allocation model that gives priority to commuting demand. These approaches improve parking reliability and reduce uncertainty before arrival. However, reservation is treated as a separate service placed on top of parking lot selection. The internal travel from the parking lot entrance to a specific reserved spot, and from that spot to a pedestrian exit, is not represented in any of them. This is the internal structure that DPSOF adds, since each available spot is modeled as a graph node connected through weighted internal driving and walking links rather than as part of an undivided parking lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

Parking allocation has received considerable attention because the available parking facilities have to be distributed among competing drivers. Tran Thi Kim et al.~\cite{p1-ref11} formulated parking assignment as a problem of minimizing parking expenses while balancing demand among several parking lots. Tang et al.~\cite{p1-ref10} introduced the SCP-tree method for identifying multiple nearby parking spots while reducing group travel cost. Hassija et al.~\cite{p1-ref9} ~\cite{p2-ref27} used virtual voting and adaptive pricing to support decentralized slot allocation. Abdelmagid et al.~\cite{p2-ref5} proposed a dynamic modeling framework for real-time parking allocation, while Amidu et al.~\cite{p2-ref7} developed a primal-dual algorithm for online parking allocation. Zheng et al.~\cite{p2-ref8} examined parking space allocation under peak demand, and Hu et al.~\cite{p2-ref10} used multi-objective intelligent optimization for parking space allocation. Although these studies improve parking utilization, fairness, and demand distribution across multiple parking lots, they do not consider a dynamic recommendation process in which parking decisions are repeatedly recomputed using the vehicle's current location together with updated traffic and parking conditions. The frameworks are built around this repeated recomputation, since the route and parking lot decision is reevaluated at every intersection the driver reaches instead of being fixed at the start of the route.

\vspace{0.18in}

Real-time and automated parking management systems have also been proposed. Kulkarni et al.~\cite{p2-ref6} developed a smart automated and real-time parking management system, while Bhimavarapu et al.~\cite{p2-ref23} presented an end-to-end system that combines real-time parking space detection and path finding. Xie et al.~\cite{p2-ref21} introduced a self-optimization method for multi-style smart parking using experience, knowledge, and data. Baikani et al.~\cite{p2-ref25} proposed NeuroPark Guide as an artificial-intelligence-based parking guidance system. These systems show that automation and adaptive decision-making are practical. However, they treat detection, guidance, or system management as separate concerns rather than combining route-dependent driving time, walking time, parking cost, and individual parking spot characteristics within one objective function. The frameworks instead evaluate all of these factors together within a single weighted objective, first at the parking lot level and then at the parking spot level.

\vspace{0.18in}

The supply of parking facilities under uncertainty has also been investigated. Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref16} formulated the dynamic supply of parking permits as a stochastic control problem. Ahmed et al.~\cite{p1-ref8} proposed a paging-based method for improving the utilization of smart parking systems. Nakazato et al.~\cite{p2-ref11} investigated parking lot allocation using rematching and dynamic parking-fee design. These approaches show that parking supply, rematching, and pricing can be adjusted to changing demand. However, the resulting decisions remain at the system level or the parking lot level. None of them connects the selected parking lot to an individual available parking spot through internal vehicle and pedestrian links, which is the extension provided by DPSOF.

\vspace{0.18in}

Beyond parking lot recommendation, several studies have considered path planning and guidance to individual parking spaces. Cheng and Kang~\cite{p2-ref15} applied a Genetic Algorithm to the path-planning problem for automatic navigation to parking spaces inside parking lots. Dong et al.~\cite{p2-ref18} developed a chaotic Particle Swarm Optimization method for parking space guidance, while Wang et al.~\cite{p2-ref19} used parallel PSO for parking guidance in complex parking environments. Cheng and Nian~\cite{p2-ref14} examined parking lot optimization using a Genetic Algorithm, and Adeyiga et al.~\cite{p2-ref20} compared GA and PSO in an intelligent parking system. These studies confirm that search-based methods can guide a vehicle toward a parking space. However, in each of these studies, the parking spot is selected only after the parking lot has already been identified, without jointly considering both recommendation levels. The parking spot is not modeled as a separate graph node with its own availability, spot-specific parking cost, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and pedestrian-exit connection. DPSOF represents this missing decision layer by modeling every currently available spot as a separate graph node with its own cost and traversal attributes.

\vspace{0.18in}

Other adaptive methods have been developed for dynamic parking guidance and street-parking optimization. Hassoune et al.~\cite{p2-ref22} proposed a dynamic parking-guidance architecture based on Ant Colony Optimization and multi-agent systems. Akandwanaho and Govender~\cite{p2-ref24} used a memetic algorithm for dynamic street parking space optimization. Dimlo et al.~\cite{p2-ref17} developed a GA-RBF parking-management model intended to reduce fuel consumption, travel time, and parking cost. These methods support adaptive and scalable parking guidance. However, their objectives and network representations remain at the lot level or the street-segment level. None of them evaluates currently available spots through both external road network travel and internal parking facility movement, which is the problem that DPSOF is formulated to solve.

\vspace{0.18in}

From a solution-method perspective, optimization models are useful for obtaining optimal solutions and for validating parking formulations on small instances. Multi-objective parking allocation~\cite{p2-ref4}~\cite{p2-ref10}, real-time parking allocation~\cite{p2-ref5}, online allocation~\cite{p2-ref7}, peak-demand allocation~\cite{p2-ref8}, and demand balancing across multiple parking lots~\cite{p1-ref11} show how mathematical and algorithmic formulations can represent parking decisions. However, as the number of nodes, parking facilities, routes, and parking spots increases, the number of possible decisions grows rapidly, and none of these formulations is evaluated for the repeated, current location-based recomputation that a moving vehicle requires. For this reason, optimization is used only for small-scale validation in this thesis, while scalable heuristic and meta-heuristic baselines are developed for medium- and large-scale networks.

\vspace{0.18in}

Heuristic and meta-heuristic methods are commonly used when optimization becomes computationally expensive. Ben Helal et al.~\cite{p1-ref18} developed a hybrid GA for a green vehicle-routing problem, which shows the suitability of GA for transportation and routing optimization. Hu and Ma~\cite{p1-ref19} applied PSO to task assignment and path planning, while Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref20} used an improved ACO method for congestion-aware transportation-route optimization. Within parking research, GA has been used for parking lot selection~\cite{p2-ref14}, path planning to parking spaces~\cite{p2-ref15}, and fuel-time-cost optimization~\cite{p2-ref17}. PSO has been applied to parking space guidance~\cite{p2-ref18}~\cite{p2-ref19}, and ACO has been used for dynamic parking guidance~\cite{p2-ref22}. These studies confirm that GA, PSO, and ACO are suitable baselines for scalable parking optimization. However, the current literature does not jointly addresses the repeated recommendation of a parking lot under changing traffic conditions and the recommendation of an individual parking spot within the selected parking lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

Despite these advances, the existing research directions are often studied separately. parking lot recommendation methods generally treat each lot as a single parking option, while parking space studies usually begin only after the lot has already been selected, and limited attention has been given to a connected framework that first updates the parking lot recommendation from the driver's current location and then extends the decision to an individual available parking spot. The parking spots are also not fully represented, since internal driving time from the entrance to the spot, internal walking time from the spot to a pedestrian exit, external walking time from the exit to the final destination, and spot-specific parking cost are rarely considered together. In addition, few studies compare optimization, heuristic, and meta-heuristic methods under the same graph structure, parameters, constraints, and cost components.

\vspace{0.18in}

These limitations motivate the two frameworks developed in this thesis. DPLOF repeatedly updates the route and the parking lot recommendation. DPSOF then extends this process by representing currently available parking spots as separate decision options, so that parking recommendation is addressed at both the parking lot level and the parking spot level. \chapter{Graph Creation}

\begin{thesisbody}

To evaluate the performance of the proposed parking recommendation frameworks, a set of different road networks is used. This set includes graphs that are synthetically generated urban-like road networks and a real-road network case study developed in Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO). The synthetic graphs are used to examine the behavior of the frameworks under controlled network sizes and parameter variations, while the SUMO-based graph is used to evaluate the frameworks on a more realistic road network.

\vspace{0.18in}

The evaluation begins with the construction of the synthetic road networks and is followed by the use of the SUMO-based real-road network for fixed network analysis. To support both parking lot level and parking spot level recommendations, parking lots, parking spots, source and destination nodes, edge distances, travel times, walking distances, and parking costs are all defined as part of the graph-creation process. This chapter presents the graph generation methodology and describes the main components used to build the experimental environment for evaluating the proposed frameworks.

\section{Synthetic Graph Generation}

The synthetic graphs are generated as directed urban-like road networks at different network sizes. They are used as controlled test cases for evaluating the proposed parking recommendation frameworks. Each graph is generated using node locations and edge links that resemble the structure of a realistic urban road network. A graph consists of nodes and directed edges, where the nodes show intersections or locations and the edges represent road connections between two nodes. Initially, the nodes are randomly generated and assigned coordinates within the network area so that the resulting road network does not follow a regular grid pattern. After the nodes are generated, directed edges are assigned as road connections between them. Some edges connect nearby nodes, while the remaining edges connect nodes that are farther apart. This creates multiple possible routes for a driver to travel from a source node to a destination node, which is similar to traveling in an urban road network.
The synthetic datasets are generated as small-, medium-, and large-scale road networks. As the network size increases, additional nodes and edges are added to the graph. The network area is also scaled so that the graph does not become too crowded. This helps to preserve a comparable road structure across the small-, medium-, and large-scale cases. As a result, each graph becomes larger and more complex than the previous one, while the generated graphs remain structurally consistent across the different network sizes.

\section{Real-Life Road Network}

Alongside the synthetic graphs, a real-road network graph developed using SUMO is used to evaluate the proposed frameworks under realistic road network conditions. The road network data were obtained from OpenStreetMap through Overpass Turbo and were then imported into SUMO for simulation and preprocessing. Unlike the synthetic networks, which are generated artificially for controlled experiments, the SUMO-based graph preserves the actual road layout and connectivity of the selected area. This makes it more suitable for examining how the model behaves under realistic road conditions. The SUMO road network is then converted into a graph representation in which intersections and other relevant road points are treated as nodes, and road segments are treated as directed edges. This produces a directed road graph that can be used within the same route and parking framework as a synthetic network. The SUMO-based graph used in this thesis contains 2055 nodes and serves as the fixed real-road network for the experimental analysis presented later. Since it is derived from an actual road map, it provides a more realistic setting than synthetic cases.

\section{Parking Lot Positioning}

Parking lots are represented as candidate parking nodes in the driving network. Their placement is important because the problem studied in this thesis is not only a routing problem. The driver first travels by vehicle to a selected parking lot and then walks from that parking lot to the final destination.

\vspace{0.18in}

In the baseline setting, approximately \(5\%\) of the graph nodes are selected as candidate parking locations. These locations are selected near the destination region so that the parking decision remains meaningful. This follows the practical assumption that drivers usually look for parking close to their final destination.

\vspace{0.18in}

For the parameter sensitivity analysis, the number of candidate parking locations is varied by changing the parking-supply percentage, while the other parameters are kept fixed. This allows the effect of parking availability on route selection, walking distance, parking cost, and recommendation quality to be examined separately.

\section{Parking Spot Generation and Positioning}

After the selection of parking lots, parking spots are generated for each candidate parking lot. This step supports the parking spot level recommendation framework. While the parking lot level recommendation framework selects a suitable parking lot, the spot level recommendation framework selects a suitable individual spot inside the selected lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

Each parking lot is assigned variable number of parking spots rather than a fixed count. In the baseline setting, the number of spots per lot is generated within the range of 20 to 40, and approximately \(10\%\) of the generated spots are marked as available. Each parking lot is guaranteed to contain at least one available spot, which avoids cases in which a lot is included in the candidate set but contains no feasible option.

\vspace{0.18in}

Each parking spot is assigned its own attributes, such as availability, spot-specific parking cost, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and proximity to the pedestrian exit. These attributes allow the model to compare different parking spots within the same parking lot. As a result, the recommendation process considers both the selected parking lot and the assigned parking spot.

\vspace{0.18in}

Parking costs are assigned within controlled ranges. In the baseline setting, the parking cost varies from $\$8.0$ to $\$20.5$. Additional experiments use wider cost ranges to represent stronger variation in parking prices. This helps to examine how parking cost variation affects the final recommendation.

\vspace{0.18in}

For each parking candidate, the walking distance from the parking location to the destination is calculated using the spatial layout of the graph. A parking location is considered feasible only if this walking distance is within the maximum walking threshold \(W_{\max}\). Therefore, the graph may contain several candidate parking locations, but only the feasible ones are treated as valid options for the driver.

\section{Driver Source and Destination Positioning}

The source and destination are selected so that each experiment produces a meaningful parking recommendation scenario. In the synthetic graphs, the source node is selected from the driving network and is placed far enough from the destination region. This avoids very short routes and ensures that both driving and walking decisions affect the final result.

\vspace{0.18in}

Destination is placed near the outer region of the network instead of being treated as an ordinary internal node. This supports the two-stage travel structure used in this thesis. In the first stage, driver travels by vehicle from source to a selected parking lot. In the second stage, driver walks from parking lot to final destination. For each scenario, the same source and destination are used for all compared methods. This ensures that the optimization model, the heuristic method, and the meta-heuristic methods are evaluated under the same travel conditions. Therefore, any difference in performance is caused by the decision method rather than by any change in source and destination.

\section{Edge Weight and travel time Assignment}

Each directed road edge is assigned a distance and a travel time. Distance is calculated from the spatial positions of two connected nodes. Travel time is then calculated from this distance and driving speed assigned to that road segment.

\vspace{0.18in}

In synthetic networks, different road types are used to represent local roads, connector roads, and arterial roads. This introduces variation in travel time structure and makes the generated graphs more realistic than uniform random graphs. In baseline setting, low traffic driving speeds are sampled from \(50\) to \(70\,\mathrm{km/h}\). The other traffic scenarios use different speed ranges to represent very low, medium, and high traffic conditions. When traffic condition changes, travel times are recomputed while road topology remains fixed. This allows the effect of traffic variation to be studied without changing the structure of the graph.

\vspace{0.18in}

Walking travel is handled separately from vehicle travel. A fixed walking speed of \(5.0\,\mathrm{km/h}\) is used to convert walking distance into walking time. This calculation is used in both parking lot level and parking spot level recommendations, respectively. For the edge density analysis, additional bidirectional driving links are added between eligible node pairs, while the other parameters remain fixed. The resulting number of directed edges is used to represent the edge density setting in the experimental results.

\section{Summary}

This chapter describes the graph creation process used in this thesis. Synthetic urban-like directed graphs are generated at different scales to support controlled evaluation, and a SUMO-based real-road network case study is utilized to test the frameworks on a more realistic road structure. Parking lots are modeled as candidate nodes near the destination region, and parking spots are generated inside the selected parking lots to support spot level recommendation. The source and destination are placed so that the two-stage travel process of driving to a parking lot and then walking to the final destination is preserved. Finally, distances, travel times, walking times, and parking costs are assigned to support the evaluation of both parking lot level and parking spot level recommendations. These graph components provide the basis for the experimental analysis presented in the following chapters.

\end{thesisbody}

\chapter{Proposed Dynamic Parking Optimization Framework for Parking Lot Selection}
\begin{thesisbody}

Parking lot search contributes to congestion, travel delay, and inefficient use of road infrastructure, especially in dense urban areas. Existing parking recommendation approaches may become less effective when traffic conditions change while the driver is still driving toward the recommended parking lot. To address this limitation, this chapter proposes a Dynamic Parking Lot Optimization Framework (DPLOF) that updates the traffic information and recalculates the route and the parking lot recommendation from vehicle's current location. The objective is to minimize the total travel time, which includes both driving time and walking time, together with the parking cost, while satisfying user preferences such as maximum walking distance and maximum parking cost. An optimization model is proposed to validate the formulation on small-scale networks, while a heuristic approach is developed for medium- and large-scale networks where solving the optimization model repeatedly becomes computationally impractical. The heuristic is then evaluated against meta-heuristic baselines, namely Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), using several performance metrics under controlled variations in network and parking conditions. The evaluation is carried out using synthetic urban-like datasets and a SUMO-based real-road network case study for practical validation. The results suggest that DPLOF is suitable for real-time parking lot level recommendation in urban environments.

\section{Introduction}

Rapid growth of population and vehicle ownership in dense urban areas has made parking a persistent challenge in smart transportation systems. Drivers often spend a considerable amount of time searching for a parking lot near their destination, which increases congestion, travel delay, fuel consumption, emissions, and driver frustration \cite{p1-ref1}. Therefore, efficient parking management is important for smart transportation systems, since it directly influences congestion mitigation, travel time reduction, and overall road network performance \cite{p1-ref2}. For this reason, Smart Parking Systems (SPS) have been developed to improve the monitoring, estimation, and prediction of parking space occupancy \cite{p1-ref3}. In comparison with traditional search methods, these systems help drivers identify parking options more efficiently \cite{p1-ref4}.

\vspace{0.18in}

However, when a driver is already moving through a road network, selecting a suitable parking lot requires more than identifying parking availability alone. The decision has to minimize the total travel time, which includes the driving time from the current location to the parking lot and the walking time from the selected lot to the destination, together with the parking cost, while also considering the current traffic condition and the user preferences. These factors show that parking lot recommendation is affected by both route-related conditions and parking system characteristics. Prior studies further show that reservation policies \cite{p1-ref5}, competition among parking facilities \cite{p1-ref6}, and pricing mechanisms \cite{p1-ref7} can influence parking access, demand distribution, utilization, and selection behavior. These studies form the basis for a broader body of SPS research that addresses different aspects of parking management.

\vspace{0.18in}

Existing SPS research has addressed several important functions, including allocation, pricing, assignment, monitoring, reservation, and demand estimation. These studies show the importance of space utilization \cite{p1-ref8}, adaptive pricing under changing demand \cite{p1-ref9}, travel cost among nearby parking lots \cite{p1-ref10}, demand balancing across parking lots \cite{p1-ref11}, and occupancy estimation under incomplete sensing infrastructure \cite{p1-ref12}. Other works have considered transaction-based occupancy inference \cite{p1-ref13}, reservation before arrival \cite{p1-ref14}, parking space-view-based service improvement \cite{p1-ref15}, uncertain parking availability \cite{p1-ref16}, and curb parking demand influenced by nearby buildings, businesses, and activity areas \cite{p1-ref17}. Despite these advances, limited attention has been given to parking lot recommendation methods that update both the route and the parking decision while the vehicle is already on route.

\vspace{0.18in}

To address this gap, this chapter proposes DPLOF for real-time parking lot level recommendation under changing traffic conditions. DPLOF performs repeated route and parking lot recomputation during travel using continuously updated real-time traffic information. At each intersection, the system evaluates the available parking lots through an objective function that minimizes the total travel time, including both driving time and walking time, together with their respective parking cost. Based on this evaluation, the route and the parking lot recommendation are recomputed from the current vehicle location until the vehicle reaches the selected lot. This process avoids any dependence on future traffic prediction or parking-price estimation. DPLOF follows a two-level route and parking lot decision process in which the driver first travels from the current vehicle location to the selected parking lot and then walks from that lot to the destination. The recommendation also satisfies user preferences such as maximum walking distance and maximum parking cost. In this work, at least one parking lot is assumed to be available, and the parking cost is treated as a fixed value for each lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

To solve the proposed parking lot level recommendation problem, this chapter first presents an optimization model for small-scale networks. However, since repeated route and parking lot recomputation become computationally expensive as the network size increases, a heuristic solution approach is developed for medium- and large-scale networks in order to support real-time recommendation under changing traffic conditions. The proposed heuristic is evaluated against three meta-heuristic baselines, namely GA, PSO, and ACO. GA is widely used for complex routing and transportation optimization problems \cite{p1-ref18}, PSO has been applied to path planning and search-based optimization problems \cite{p1-ref19}, and ACO has been used for congestion-aware route optimization and related transportation problems \cite{p1-ref20}.

\vspace{0.18in}

The proposed approach is evaluated using controlled synthetic urban-like datasets across different performance metrics and parameter variations. The evaluation considers driving time, walking time, parking cost, objective value and runtime under controlled variations in network size, edge density, parking supply, walking distance threshold, traffic level, parking cost threshold, and parking cost range. A SUMO-based real-road network instance is also used to validate the framework under a realistic urban topology. The main contributions of this chapter are as follows:

\vspace{0.18in}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A dynamic parking lot optimization framework is proposed to minimize driving time, walking time, and parking cost under user-defined constraints by updating the traffic information and recomputing the route and the parking lot recommendation at each intersection without relying on future conditions.

    \item An optimization model is proposed for small-scale validation, and a heuristic solution approach is developed for medium-scale and large-scale networks where solving the optimization model repeatedly becomes computationally expensive.

    \item The proposed heuristic is evaluated against GA, PSO, and ACO using travel time, walking distance, parking cost, and runtime under controlled parameter variations, including network size, edge density, parking supply, traffic level, walking distance threshold, parking cost threshold, and parking cost range.

    \item A SUMO-based real-road network instance is used to validate the framework under a realistic urban topology.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:dplof_system_model} presents the system model. Section~\ref{sec:dplof_formulation} details the problem formulation. Section~\ref{sec:dplof_methods} describes the proposed heuristic and the meta-heuristic baselines. Section~\ref{sec:dplof_results} presents the experimental setup, the synthetic evaluation, the performance analysis, and the SUMO-based validation. Section~\ref{sec:dplof_conclusion} concludes the chapter and outlines future perspectives.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:dplof_related_work}

Although Chapter~\ref{ch:related_work} reviewed the existing literature in detail, this subsection focuses specifically on studies related to the parking lot recommendation problem. Prior work has shown that route quality, parking supply, traffic conditions, and pricing all influence the outcome of a parking lot recommendation. This review therefore highlights the main factors and methodological trends in the related works that define the parking lot recommendation problem studied in this chapter.

\vspace{0.18in}

SPS have emerged as an important solution for reducing the time drivers spend searching for parking in urban areas. Traditional parking systems provide limited support and often require drivers to search manually for an available parking lot or space, which increases congestion, fuel consumption, emissions, and travel delay. With the development of Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), prior research has improved parking-related services through occupancy prediction, pricing strategies, reservation services, allocation models, and monitoring methods. However, these studies often address individual components of the parking problem rather than the complete parking lot level recommendation process for a driver who is already moving through an urban road network.

\vspace{0.18in}

A major direction in the literature focuses on parking occupancy prediction. Accurate prediction of vacant parking spaces can improve parking guidance and reduce unnecessary search time. Li et al.~\cite{p1-ref1} proposed a near-real-time method for predicting parking occupancy across different parking lots, and their later work improved the prediction accuracy by capturing the relationships among nearby parking areas and the occupancy changes over time~\cite{p1-ref2}. Zhao et al.~\cite{p1-ref3} used parking meters as virtual sensors to estimate on-street parking availability at the urban scale, and Song et al.~\cite{p1-ref4} addressed missing sensor data through collaborative vacancy prediction. These studies improve parking availability estimation, but they mainly focus on prediction and sensing rather than on repeated route and parking lot recomputation using current traffic information while the vehicle is already on route.

\vspace{0.18in}

Another research stream focuses on parking pricing, competition, and reservation management. Boateng et al.~\cite{p1-ref5} proposed a reservation and pricing framework for automated valet parking and charging using multi-agent reinforcement learning. Saharan et al.~\cite{p1-ref6} developed a dynamic pricing and allocation model for smart on-street parking, Hu et al.~\cite{p1-ref7} studied parking management under demand uncertainty, and Ahmed et al.~\cite{p1-ref8} proposed a paging-based reservation strategy to improve parking-slot utilization. These studies show that pricing and reservation mechanisms can improve system-level parking management. However, their primary focus is on the operation of the parking system rather than on driver-centered parking lot level recommendation that jointly evaluates route-dependent driving time, walking time, parking cost, and user preferences.

\vspace{0.18in}

Parking allocation and assignment have also been widely studied. Hassija et al.~\cite{p1-ref9} proposed a decentralized parking-slot allocation framework using virtual voting and adaptive pricing to improve fairness and security. Tang et al.~\cite{p1-ref10} introduced a spatial index tree, called the SCP-tree, to identify multiple nearby parking spots while reducing group travel cost, and Tran Thi Kim et al.~\cite{p1-ref11} formulated parking assignment as a demand-balancing problem across multiple parking lots. These works show the importance of fair allocation, reduced travel cost, and balanced use of parking facilities. However, they generally do not model parking lot level recommendation as a repeated two-level route and parking decision in which the driving route is updated from the current vehicle location under changing traffic conditions.

\vspace{0.18in}

Several studies have focused on parking monitoring, demand estimation, occupancy inference, and reservation support. Kim et al.~\cite{p1-ref12} explored the use of unmanned aerial vehicles for parking occupancy monitoring. Assemi et al.~\cite{p1-ref13} inferred parking occupancy from payment transaction data when complete sensing infrastructure is unavailable. Hu et al.~\cite{p1-ref14} examined competition and reservation services in smart parking systems. Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref15} proposed a reservation optimization method based on a parking space view, Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref16} studied the dynamic supply of parking spaces under uncertainty, and Nie et al.~\cite{p1-ref17} estimated public street-parking demand using nearby activity locations such as commercial areas, offices, and public facilities. These studies improve parking data collection, occupancy estimation, demand understanding, and reservation support. However, they mainly strengthen individual SPS functions rather than combining current traffic information, route-dependent driving time, walking distance feasibility, parking cost, and user preferences within a single real-time parking lot level recommendation framework.

\vspace{0.18in}

Since parking lot level recommendation in urban networks involves a large number of possible route and parking combinations, heuristic and meta-heuristic methods are often used in transportation optimization. Ben Helal et al.~\cite{p1-ref18} showed the effectiveness of a hybrid GA for vehicle routing. Hu et al.~\cite{p1-ref19} used PSO for path planning in complex environments, and Wang et al.~\cite{p1-ref20} developed an improved ACO approach for congestion-aware routing. These studies show that GA, PSO, and ACO are relevant baselines for large-scale routing and search-based optimization problems. However, they are not designed for the proposed parking lot level recommendation setting, where the solution has to combine current-location-based route recomputation, parking lot selection, walking distance feasibility, and parking cost under the same objective structure.

\vspace{0.18in}

Overall, existing research has improved parking prediction, pricing, allocation, monitoring, reservation, and transportation optimization. However, these directions are often studied separately and do not fully address real-time parking lot level recommendation for a vehicle that is already moving through the road network. Limited attention has been given to methods that repeatedly recompute both the route and the parking lot recommendation from the current vehicle location using updated traffic information, while considering walking time, walking distance constraints, parking cost, and user preferences without relying on future traffic prediction or future parking-price estimation. Motivated by this gap, the proposed DPLOF formulates parking lot level recommendation as a two-level route and parking lot optimization problem, uses an optimization model for small-scale validation, develops a scalable heuristic for medium-scale and large-scale networks, and evaluates the heuristic against GA, PSO, and ACO using synthetic urban-like datasets and a SUMO-based real-road network instance.

\section{System Model and Problem Description}
\label{sec:dplof_system_model}

This section describes the system model of the proposed DPLOF. The framework consists of a driver, a centralized recommendation server, real-time traffic information, parking lot information, and an urban road network. These components work together to recommend a suitable parking lot under changing traffic conditions. In this work, at least one parking lot is assumed to be available, and the parking cost is assigned for each parking lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

The urban environment is modeled as a directed graph \(G=(N,E)\), where \(N\) denotes the set of road network nodes and \(E\) denotes the set of directed edges. The nodes represent road intersections, the driver's source node \(S\), the current vehicle location \(A\), the destination node \(D\), and the parking lots \(p_i \in P\), where \(P\) is the set of parking lots. Each directed edge \((i,j)\in E\) represents a road segment and is associated with a fixed travel distance \(d_{ij}\) and a travel time \(t_{ij}\). The distance \(d_{ij}\) remains fixed, while \(t_{ij}\) changes according to the current traffic condition. Therefore, the road network structure remains fixed during the recommendation, but the edge travel times are updated as the traffic condition changes.

\vspace{0.18in}

DPLOF follows a two-level route and parking lot decision process. At the first level, the driver travels from the current vehicle location \(A\) to a selected parking lot \(p_i\). At the second level, the driver walks from the selected parking lot to the destination \(D\). Let \(t^{drive}_{p_i}\) denote the estimated driving time from the current vehicle location \(A\) to parking lot \(p_i\) under the current traffic condition. The walking distance from \(p_i\) to \(D\) is denoted by \(d^{walk}_{p_i}\). Since the walking speed is assumed to be constant, the corresponding walking time \(t^{walk}_{p_i}\) can be derived from \(d^{walk}_{p_i}\) when required.

\vspace{0.18in}

As the vehicle moves through the road network, the centralized recommendation server updates the traffic state and recomputes the route and the parking lot recommendation from the current vehicle location. When the vehicle reaches a new intersection, the system retrieves the latest traffic and parking lot information, evaluates the parking lots using the objective function, and updates the recommended route and parking lot. This repeated recomputation continues until the vehicle reaches the selected parking lot. The model uses current traffic information only and does not forecast future traffic conditions or future parking-price changes.

\vspace{0.18in}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dplof_system_model} illustrates the system workflow as an example. The parking lots \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) represent the parking lot options near the destination. The quantities \(t^{drive}_{p_1}\) and \(t^{drive}_{p_2}\) denote the driving times from the current vehicle location to the respective parking lots, \(t^{walk}_{p_1}\) and \(t^{walk}_{p_2}\) denote the walking times from those parking lots to the destination, and \(C_{p_1}\) and \(C_{p_2}\) denote the parking costs. As the driver progresses through the road network, the system updates the traffic information and re-evaluates the route and the parking lot recommendation using the total travel time, the parking cost, and the user preferences.

    \caption{System model of the proposed dynamic parking lot optimization framework.}
    \label{fig:dplof_system_model}

\FloatBarrier

Each parking lot \(p_i\) is characterized by two main attributes, which are the walking distance \(d^{walk}_{p_i}\) and the parking cost \(C_{p_i}\). Parking availability is assumed in this work, and each generated instance includes at least one parking lot that satisfies the user preferences. A parking lot is excluded from consideration if its walking distance exceeds the maximum acceptable walking distance \(W_{\max}\) or if its parking cost exceeds the maximum acceptable parking cost \(C_{\max}\). Therefore, the feasible parking lot set is determined by the user-defined walking distance and parking cost constraints.

\vspace{0.18in}

At each decision step, the system evaluates the tuple
\(
(t_{p_i}^{\mathrm{drive}},\allowbreak
t_{p_i}^{\mathrm{walk}},\allowbreak
C_{p_i},\allowbreak
d_{p_i}^{\mathrm{walk}})
\)
for every \(p_i \in P\). The parking lots that satisfy the walking distance and parking cost constraints are retained as feasible options. These feasible parking lots are then evaluated by the optimization model, the proposed heuristic, and the meta-heuristic baselines under the same system model. The recommendation process is repeated as the driver moves from one intersection to another until the selected parking lot is reached.

\vspace{0.18in}

Several assumptions are made in the system model. The urban road network structure and the edge distances remain fixed during the recommendation interval, while the edge travel times may change according to the current traffic condition. The walking distance from each parking lot to the destination remain fixed. Parking availability is assumed, and at least one feasible parking lot is included in each generated instance. The model uses current traffic, parking lot price, and future traffic conditions and future parking-price changes are not forecasted. \section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:dplof_formulation}

This section formulates parking lot level recommendation in DPLOF as a two-level route and parking lot optimization problem over the network defined in the system model. The objective is to determine a route in which the driver first travels by vehicle from the current location to a selected parking lot and then walks from that lot to the destination, while jointly minimizing the total travel time and the parking cost under user-defined constraints. At the initial decision step, the starting node corresponds to source node \(S\). During route progression, the same formulation is reevaluated by replacing \(S\) with driver's current node \(A\) whenever vehicle reaches a new intersection. In this structure, destination is not reached directly via driving network. Instead, driving route terminates at a selected parking lot, and final connection to destination is completed through a parking to destination walking edge.

\subsection{Network Representation and Decision Variables}

Based on the network representation in the system model, decision variables are introduced for route and parking lot selection. Using \(G=(N,E)\), binary route-selection variables are defined over \(E\), which contains both driving edges and parking to destination walking edges. For each \((i,j)\in E\), the binary decision variable in Equation~\eqref{eq:x_definition} is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
x_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if edge } (i,j) \text{ is included in the selected route},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:x_definition}
\end{equation}

Let \(T_i\) denote the accumulated travel time upon reaching node \(i \in N\). Since the route includes both the driving path to a parking lot and the final walking edge to the destination, the accumulated value at node \(D\), denoted by \(T_D\), represents the total travel time from the starting node to the destination. As described in the system model, let \(P\) denote the set of parking lots considered during the recommendation, and let \(L[p]\) denote the graph node corresponding to \(p\). The selected parking lot is identified through the walking edge \((L[p],D)\). The parameter \(C[p]\) represents the parking cost associated with \(p\), and the variable \(Cost\) denotes the parking cost of the selected lot. In the implementation, an auxiliary parking-selection variable is linked to \((L[p],D)\). However, the formulation is presented directly using the route variable associated with the final walking edge.

\subsection{Objective Function}

The objective function in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} minimizes a weighted combination of total travel time and parking cost:

\begin{equation}
\min \; \alpha T_D + (1-\alpha) Cost,
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

where \(T_D\) denotes the total travel time, including both driving and walking times, \(Cost\) is the parking cost of the selected parking lot, and \(\alpha \in [0,1]\) is the objective weight parameter that controls the trade-off between travel time and parking cost. Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} therefore balances travel efficiency and parking cost while reflecting the user preferences.

\subsection{Routing Feasibility Constraints}

The selected edges have to form a valid and continuous route from the starting node to a parking lot and then from that parking lot to the destination. To enforce this structure, the flow-conservation constraint in Equation~\eqref{eq:flow_conservation} is imposed over the network:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E} x_{ij} - \sum_{j:(j,i)\in E} x_{ji} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } i = S,\\
-1, & \text{if } i = D,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:flow_conservation}
\end{equation}

The net outgoing flow at \(S\) is set to 1, which shows that the route begins at the source or current node, while the net incoming flow at \(D\) is set to to -1, which shows that the route terminates at the destination. All intermediate nodes satisfy balanced flow. Since the destination is reachable only through a parking to destination walking edge, Equation~\eqref{eq:flow_conservation} ensures that the route first reaches a parking lot through the driving network and then arrives at the destination through the final walking connection.

\vspace{0.18in}

To maintain route consistency, Equation~\eqref{eq:bidirectional_constraint} prevents the route from using both directions of the same bidirectional road segment:

\begin{equation}
x_{ij} + x_{ji} \leq 1, \qquad \forall (i,j)\in E \text{ such that } (j,i)\in E.
\label{eq:bidirectional_constraint}
\end{equation}

\subsection{travel time Accumulation}

The accumulation of travel time along the route is modeled in Equation~\eqref{eq:time_accumulation}:

\begin{equation}
x_{hi}=1 \;\Rightarrow\; T_i = T_h + t_{hi}, \qquad \forall (h,i)\in E,\; i\neq S.
\label{eq:time_accumulation}
\end{equation}

Here, \(t_{hi}\) represents the travel time on edge \((h,i)\) under the current traffic condition. For a selected edge, the accumulated travel time at node \(i\) has to equal the accumulated travel time at node \(h\) plus the travel time associated with edge \((h,i)\). Since the edge set includes both driving edges and the final parking to destination walking edge, the same propagation rule applies to both levels of the route.

\vspace{0.18in}

The starting-node time is initialized using Equation~\eqref{eq:source_time}:

\begin{equation}
T_S = 0.
\label{eq:source_time}
\end{equation}

As a result, \(T_D\) represents the total travel time of the complete route.

\subsection{Parking Selection and Cost Constraints}

The selected parking lot is determined through the final parking to destination walking edge. Since the complete route reaches the destination through exactly one such edge, the parking-selection constraint in Equation~\eqref{eq:parking_selection} ensures that a single parking lot is selected:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{p\in P:(L[p],D)\in E} x_{L[p],D} = 1.
\label{eq:parking_selection}
\end{equation}

The parking cost variable is linked to the selected parking lot using Equation~\eqref{eq:parking_cost_link}:

\begin{equation}
Cost = \sum_{p\in P:(L[p],D)\in E} C[p]\,x_{L[p],D}.
\label{eq:parking_cost_link}
\end{equation}

This ensures that \(Cost\) is equal to the parking cost of the selected parking lot. The user's maximum parking cost preference is then enforced using Equation~\eqref{eq:parking_cost_cap}:

\begin{equation}
Cost \leq C_{\max}.
\label{eq:parking_cost_cap}
\end{equation}

Together, Equations~\eqref{eq:parking_selection} to~\eqref{eq:parking_cost_cap} ensure that exactly one parking lot is selected and that its cost satisfies the user's parking cost constraint. In this work, at least one parking lot is assumed to be available at each recommendation step.

\raggedbottom
\subsection{Maximum walking distance Constraint}

To satisfy the user's walking preference, the walking distance from the selected parking lot to the destination has to remain within the maximum acceptable walking distance, as enforced by Equation~\eqref{eq:walking_distance_constraint}:

\begin{equation}
d^{walk}_{p}\,x_{L[p],D} \leq W_{\max}, \qquad \forall p\in P \text{ such that } (L[p],D)\in E.
\label{eq:walking_distance_constraint}
\end{equation}

Here, \(d^{walk}_{p}\) denotes the walking distance from \(p\) to the destination through the parking to destination walking edge \((L[p],D)\), and \(W_{\max}\) denotes the maximum acceptable walking distance. Equation~\eqref{eq:walking_distance_constraint} allows a parking lot to be selected only when its walking distance satisfies the user's walking distance constraint.

\vspace{0.18in}

Under the proposed formulation, a solution consists of a driving route, a selected parking lot, and a final walking edge from that lot to the destination. This structure ensures that the destination is reached through the selected parking lot rather than directly through the driving network. The formulation therefore combines route selection, travel time accumulation, parking cost control, and walking distance feasibility within a single optimization model.

\vspace{0.18in}

Since the recommendation is recomputed repeatedly as the vehicle progresses through the intersections of the road network, the computational complexity increases significantly with the network size. For this reason, the optimization model is mainly used for small-scale validation, while scalable heuristic and meta-heuristic approaches are developed for medium-scale and large-scale networks.

\section{Heuristic and Meta-Heuristic Methods}
\label{sec:dplof_methods}

This section presents the proposed heuristic and the meta-heuristic baselines used in DPLOF. The proposed heuristic is designed as the main scalable solution method for medium- and large-scale networks, where solving the optimization model repeatedly becomes computationally expensive. GA, PSO, and ACO are included as comparative baselines. All methods follow the same two-level route and parking structure, in which the driver travels by vehicle from the source or current node to a selected parking lot and then walks from that lot to the destination.

\subsection{Proposed Heuristic Method}

The proposed heuristic provides a scalable approximation of the optimization objective. Instead of solving the full optimization model at every recommendation step, it computes the shortest-path driving times from the current vehicle location to all candidate parking lots, filters out the infeasible options, removes the dominated candidates, and ranks the remaining parking lots using a weighted travel time and parking cost score. This design allows the recommendation to be recomputed efficiently when the vehicle reaches a new intersection or when the traffic information is updated.

\vspace{0.18in}

Starting from the current node \(A\), the heuristic first applies Dijkstra's algorithm using the current edge travel times. For each candidate parking lot, it obtains the driving time from \(A\), retrieves the walking distance from that lot to the destination \(D\), and converts this walking distance into walking time using the fixed walking speed \(v_{\mathrm{walk}}\). A parking lot is retained only if it is available and satisfies both \(W_{\max}\) and \(C_{\max}\). The remaining candidates are then reduced using dominance filtering. Finally, each retained parking lot is scored using the same weighted structure as the optimization objective, and the lot with the minimum score is selected. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:heuristic_method}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Proposed Heuristic Method}
\label{alg:heuristic_method}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Road network \(G\), current node \(A\), destination \(D\), candidate parking lots \(P\), edge travel times, walking distances, parking costs, availability, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), walking speed \(v_{\mathrm{walk}}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Recommended parking lot \(p^*\)
\STATE \(F \leftarrow \emptyset,\; p^* \leftarrow \emptyset,\; H^* \leftarrow \infty\)
\STATE \(\mathrm{Dist} \leftarrow \mathrm{Dijkstra}(G,A)\)
\FOR{each \(p \in P\)}
    \STATE \(t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p} \leftarrow \mathrm{Dist}[p]\)
    \STATE \(d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p} \leftarrow \mathrm{DirectWalkDistance}(p,D)\)
    \STATE \(t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p} \leftarrow d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p}/v_{\mathrm{walk}}\)
    \IF{\(a_p>0\) and \(d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p}\leq W_{\max}\) and \(C_p\leq C_{\max}\)}
        \STATE \(F \leftarrow F \cup \{p\}\)
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \(F \leftarrow \mathrm{DominancePrune}(F)\)
\FOR{each \(p \in F\)}
    \STATE \(H_p \leftarrow \alpha\left(t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p}+t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p}\right)+(1-\alpha)C_p\)
    \IF{\(H_p < H^*\)}
        \STATE \(H^* \leftarrow H_p,\; p^* \leftarrow p\)
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \(p^*\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The minimum driving time from \(A\) to each candidate parking lot \(p_i\) is computed using Dijkstra's algorithm over the current edge travel times:

\begin{equation}
t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_i}=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{Dijkstra}}(A,p_i).
\label{eq:heuristic_drive_time}
\end{equation}

The walking distance from \(p_i\) to \(D\) is obtained from the direct parking to destination walking connection:

\begin{equation}
d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}=\mathrm{DirectWalkDistance}(p_i,D).
\label{eq:heuristic_walk_distance}
\end{equation}

The corresponding walking time is derived using the fixed walking speed:

\begin{equation}
t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}=
\frac{d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}}{v_{\mathrm{walk}}}.
\label{eq:heuristic_walk_time}
\end{equation}

A parking lot is considered feasible only if it satisfies the availability, walking distance, and parking cost requirements:

\begin{equation}
a_{p_i}>0,\qquad
d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}\leq W_{\max},\qquad
C_{p_i}\leq C_{\max}.
\label{eq:heuristic_feasibility}
\end{equation}

After feasibility filtering, dominance filtering is applied to remove the inferior candidates. A parking lot \(p_i\) is dominated if another parking lot \(p_j\) is no yield higher objective values in driving time, walking distance, and parking cost, and is strictly better in at least one of these criteria:

\begin{equation}
t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_j}\leq t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_i},\quad
d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_j}\leq d^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i},\quad
C_{p_j}\leq C_{p_i}.
\label{eq:heuristic_dominance}
\end{equation}

For each remaining candidate, the total travel time is computed as the sum of the driving time and the walking time:

\begin{equation}
t^{\mathrm{total}}_{p_i}
=
t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_i}+t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}.
\label{eq:heuristic_total_time}
\end{equation}

The final heuristic score is then derived as

\begin{equation}
H_{p_i}
=
\alpha t^{\mathrm{total}}_{p_i}
+
(1-\alpha)C_{p_i}.
\label{eq:heuristic_score}
\end{equation}

The recommended parking lot is the feasible non-dominated candidate with the minimum heuristic score:

\begin{equation}
p^*
=
\arg\min_{p_i\in F} H_{p_i}.
\label{eq:heuristic_selection}
\end{equation}

Since the proposed heuristic computes the shortest-path costs once from the current node and then ranks the feasible parking lots directly, it can be recomputed efficiently as the traffic condition changes during route progression.

\subsection{Meta-Heuristic Baseline Design}

To benchmark the proposed heuristic, three meta-heuristic baselines are implemented, namely GA, PSO, and ACO. These methods follow the same feasibility constraints and objective structure as the proposed heuristic. Before any meta-heuristic search is applied, the candidate parking lots are filtered using availability, walking connection, walking distance threshold, and parking cost threshold. Each feasible solution consists of a selected parking lot and a corresponding driving route from the current node to that lot. The final walking component is then obtained from the parking to destination walking connection.

\vspace{0.18in}

Unlike the proposed heuristic, which first computes Dijkstra-based driving times and then ranks the parking lots directly, the meta-heuristic baselines perform a joint search over parking lot selection and route construction. Each method generates parking lot choices together with route-preference information. A guided Depth First Search (DFS) and backtracking decoder then constructs a valid driving route to the selected parking lot by exploring the admissible outgoing branches in the road network. If a branch cannot reach the selected lot, the decoder backtracks and explores the next admissible continuation.
The quality of each decoded route and parking solution is evaluated using the common weighted objective:

\begin{equation}
H_{p_i}
=
\alpha\left(t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_i}+t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}\right)
+
(1-\alpha)C_{p_i}.
\label{eq:meta_objective}
\end{equation}

Here, \(t^{\mathrm{drive}}_{p_i}\) is obtained from the decoded driving route, \(t^{\mathrm{walk}}_{p_i}\) is derived from the parking to destination walking connection, and \(C_{p_i}\) is the fixed cost of the selected parking lot.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm}

GA is implemented as an evolutionary baseline. Each chromosome contains a parking lot gene and a set of node-priority genes. The parking lot gene identifies the selected candidate parking lot, while the node-priority genes define the route-preference order used by the guided DFS. During each generation, the chromosomes are decoded into route and parking solutions and evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:meta_objective}. Better chromosomes are favored during selection, while crossover and mutation explore new parking lot and route combinations. Elitism is used to preserve the best solution found so far. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dplof_ga_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Genetic Algorithm Baseline}
\label{alg:dplof_ga_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Road network \(G\), source or current node, destination \(D\), feasible parking lots \(P\), edge travel times, parking costs, walking distances, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), population size \(N_{\mathrm{pop}}\), generations \(G_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Best parking lot \(p^*\) and corresponding route
\STATE \(\mathcal{P}^{(0)} \leftarrow \mathrm{InitializePopulation}(N_{\mathrm{pop}},P)\)
\STATE \((R^*,p^*,f^*) \leftarrow (\emptyset,\emptyset,\infty)\)
\FOR{\(g \leftarrow 1\) to \(G_{\max}\)}
    \FOR{each chromosome \(c \in \mathcal{P}^{(g-1)}\)}
        \STATE \(R_c \leftarrow \mathrm{GuidedDFSBacktracking}(c)\)
        \STATE \(f_c \leftarrow \mathrm{EvaluateObjective}(R_c,\alpha)\)
        \IF{\(f_c < f^*\)}
            \STATE \((R^*,p^*,f^*) \leftarrow (R_c,\mathrm{ParkingLot}(c),f_c)\)
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \(\mathcal{P}' \leftarrow \mathrm{TournamentSelect}(\mathcal{P}^{(g-1)})\)
    \STATE \(\mathcal{P}'' \leftarrow \mathrm{Crossover}(\mathcal{P}')\)
    \STATE \(\mathcal{P}''' \leftarrow \mathrm{Mutate}(\mathcal{P}'')\)
    \STATE \(\mathcal{P}^{(g)} \leftarrow \mathrm{PreserveElite}(\mathcal{P}''',R^*,p^*,f^*)\)
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \((R^*,p^*)\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimization}

PSO is implemented as a swarm-based baseline. Each particle contains a parking-position variable that maps to a feasible parking lot, together with node-priority values that guide the route construction. At each iteration, every particle is decoded into a route and parking solution and evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:meta_objective}. Each particle updates its personal-best solution when an improved solution is found, and the swarm maintains a global-best solution. The particle positions are then updated using the personal-best and global-best information. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dplof_pso_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Particle Swarm Optimization Baseline}
\label{alg:dplof_pso_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Road network \(G\), source or current node, destination \(D\), feasible parking lots \(P\), edge travel times, parking costs, walking distances, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), swarm size \(N_{\mathrm{swarm}}\), iterations \(I_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Best parking lot \(p^*\) and corresponding route
\STATE \(\mathcal{S} \leftarrow \mathrm{InitializeSwarm}(N_{\mathrm{swarm}},P)\)
\STATE \((gbest_R,gbest_p,gbest_f) \leftarrow (\emptyset,\emptyset,\infty)\)
\FOR{each particle \(u \in \mathcal{S}\)}
    \STATE \(R_u \leftarrow \mathrm{GuidedDFSBacktracking}(u)\)
    \STATE \(f_u \leftarrow \mathrm{EvaluateObjective}(R_u,\alpha)\)
    \STATE \((pbest_R[u],pbest_p[u],pbest_f[u]) \leftarrow (R_u,\mathrm{ParkingLot}(u),f_u)\)
    \IF{\(f_u < gbest_f\)}
        \STATE \((gbest_R,gbest_p,gbest_f) \leftarrow (R_u,\mathrm{ParkingLot}(u),f_u)\)
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{\(iter \leftarrow 1\) to \(I_{\max}\)}
    \FOR{each particle \(u \in \mathcal{S}\)}
        \STATE \(R_u \leftarrow \mathrm{GuidedDFSBacktracking}(u)\)
        \STATE \(f_u \leftarrow \mathrm{EvaluateObjective}(R_u,\alpha)\)
        \IF{\(f_u < pbest_f[u]\)}
            \STATE \((pbest_R[u],pbest_p[u],pbest_f[u]) \leftarrow (R_u,\mathrm{ParkingLot}(u),f_u)\)
        \ENDIF
        \IF{\(f_u < gbest_f\)}
            \STATE \((gbest_R,gbest_p,gbest_f) \leftarrow (R_u,\mathrm{ParkingLot}(u),f_u)\)
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \(\mathcal{S} \leftarrow \mathrm{UpdateParticles}(\mathcal{S},pbest,gbest)\)
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \((gbest_R,gbest_p)\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Ant Colony Optimization}

ACO is implemented as a pheromone-based baseline. Each ant selects a feasible parking lot and constructs a driving route using pheromone-guided edge preferences together with the guided DFS and backtracking decoder. After all ants have constructed their route and parking solutions, each solution is evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:meta_objective}. The best solution found so far is updated, the pheromone values are evaporated on all driving edges, and the feasible ant solutions deposit pheromone on the edges they use. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dplof_aco_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Ant Colony Optimization Baseline}
\label{alg:dplof_aco_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Road network \(G\), source or current node, destination \(D\), feasible parking lots \(P\), edge travel times, parking costs, walking distances, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), number of ants \(N_{\mathrm{ants}}\), iterations \(I_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Best parking lot \(p^*\) and corresponding route
\STATE \(\tau \leftarrow \mathrm{InitializePheromones}(E)\)
\STATE \((R^*,p^*,f^*) \leftarrow (\emptyset,\emptyset,\infty)\)
\FOR{\(iter \leftarrow 1\) to \(I_{\max}\)}
    \STATE \(\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \emptyset\)
    \FOR{\(k \leftarrow 1\) to \(N_{\mathrm{ants}}\)}
        \STATE \(p_k \leftarrow \mathrm{SelectParkingLot}(P)\)
        \STATE \(R_k \leftarrow \mathrm{GuidedDFSBacktracking}(G,\tau,p_k)\)
        \STATE \(f_k \leftarrow \mathrm{EvaluateObjective}(R_k,\alpha)\)
        \STATE \(\mathcal{A} \leftarrow \mathcal{A} \cup \{(R_k,p_k,f_k)\}\)
        \IF{\(f_k < f^*\)}
            \STATE \((R^*,p^*,f^*) \leftarrow (R_k,p_k,f_k)\)
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \(\tau \leftarrow \mathrm{EvaporatePheromones}(\tau)\)
    \STATE \(\tau \leftarrow \mathrm{UpdatePheromones}(\tau,\mathcal{A})\)
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \((R^*,p^*)\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Time Complexity Analysis}

The time complexity of the proposed heuristic and the meta-heuristic baselines is expressed in terms of the number of network nodes \(|V|\), the number of network edges \(|E|\), and the number of candidate parking lots \(|P|\).

\vspace{0.18in}

For the proposed heuristic, Dijkstra's algorithm is executed once from the current node \(A\) to all reachable nodes. This step has complexity
\[
O((|V|+|E|)\log |V|).
\]
After that, the method checks the feasibility of each candidate parking lot and computes its score, which requires \(O(|P|)\). It then applies dominance filtering among the parking-lot candidates, which in the worst case requires \(O(|P|^2)\). Therefore, the overall complexity of the proposed heuristic is
\[
O((|V|+|E|)\log |V| + |P|^2).
\]

For the meta-heuristic baselines, the dominant cost comes from repeatedly constructing and evaluating feasible routes on the network. If the cost of constructing and evaluating one route is approximated as \(O(|V|+|E|)\), then the complexity of each method can be written directly in terms of \(|V|\) and \(|E|\).

For the GA baseline, let \(N_{pop}\) denote the population size and \(G_{max}\) the number of generations. Since all individuals are evaluated in every generation, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(G_{max}N_{pop}(|V|+|E|)),
\]
in addition to lower-order costs for selection, crossover, and mutation.

\vspace{0.18in}

For the PSO baseline, let \(N_{swarm}\) denote the swarm size and \(I_{max}\) the number of iterations. Since every particle must be evaluated at every iteration, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(I_{max}N_{swarm}(|V|+|E|)),
\]
together with lower-order costs for velocity and position updates.

\vspace{0.18in}

For the ACO baseline, let \(N_{ants}\) denote the number of ants. In each iteration, every ant constructs and evaluates a route, and the pheromone values on the network edges are then updated. Hence, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(I_{max}(N_{ants}(|V|+|E|)+|E|)).
\]

This analysis shows that the proposed heuristic is computationally more efficient because it solves the shortest-path problem once and then evaluates the parking-lot candidates directly, whereas GA, PSO, and ACO repeatedly construct and evaluate routes over many generations or iterations. This also helps explain the greater runtime and runtime variability observed for the various meta-heuristic baselines used in the experiments conducted on the various scales.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:dplof_results}

\subsection{Simulation Setup, Performance Metrics, and Parameters}

This section evaluates the proposed DPLOF framework under synthetic and fixed network road scenarios. In all experiments, the same two-level recommendation structure is used, in which the driver first travels from the source node to a selected parking lot and then walks from that lot to the destination. This common setting allows all methods to be compared under identical route and parking conditions.

\vspace{0.18in}

The experiments are divided into three groups. First, small-scale instances with 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 nodes are used to compare the proposed heuristic with the optimization model. Second, medium-scale and large-scale synthetic networks with 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, and 2500 nodes are used to compare the heuristic with ACO, PSO, and GA. Third, a fixed SUMO-based urban road network with 2055 nodes is used to validate the framework on a realistic directed topology.

\vspace{0.18in}

Unless otherwise stated, the baseline configuration uses a directed road network with a parking supply equal to approximately \(5\%\) of the total nodes, a maximum walking distance \(W_{\max}=0.50\,\mathrm{km}\), an objective weight \(\alpha=0.90\), a parking budget \(C_{\max}=20.5\), and a parking cost range from \(8.0\) to \(20.5\). Under Low traffic conditions, vehicle speeds are sampled from \(50\) to \(70\,\mathrm{km/h}\), while the walking speed is fixed at \(v_{\mathrm{walk}}=5.0\,\mathrm{km/h}\).

\vspace{0.18in}

The parameter sensitivity analysis follows a controlled one-factor-at-a-time design, which is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:parameter_variation_mapping}. In each experiment, a single parameter is varied while the remaining parameters are kept at their baseline values. The walking distance analysis uses \(W_{\max}=0.25\), \(0.50\), \(0.75\), and \(1.00\,\mathrm{km}\). The parking-supply analysis uses approximately \(5\%\), \(10\%\), \(15\%\), and \(20\%\) candidate parking lots. The parking budget analysis uses \(C_{\max}=10.0\), \(15.0\), \(20.5\), and \(25.0\). The parking cost analysis uses the ranges \(8.0\)--\(20.5\), \(10.0\)--\(22.5\), \(12.5\)--\(25.0\), and \(15.0\)--\(30.0\). The traffic analysis considers very low, low, medium, and high traffic using speed ranges of \(60\)--\(80\), \(50\)--\(70\), \(30\)--\(49\), and \(10\)--\(29\,\mathrm{km/h}\), respectively. For the edge density analysis, the horizontal axis reports the actual number of directed edges in each scenario.

The evaluation is based on five performance indicators, namely driving time, walking time, parking cost, objective value, and runtime. The driving time measures the travel from the source node to the selected parking lot, while the walking time measures the movement from that lot to the final destination. These two terms together represent the total travel time. The parking cost captures the parking cost of the selected parking option. The objective value combines the total travel time and the parking cost using the weight \(\alpha\), as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective}, and is therefore the single quantity that the methods minimize. The runtime measures the computational efficiency. For every scenario, all methods are tested on the same road network, source-destination pair, parking lot candidates, walking distance limit, parking budget limit, and objective weight setting. Therefore, the observed differences can be attributed to the solution methods rather than to changes in the underlying test instance.

These instances are used to examine whether the heuristic can preserve the quality of the optimum while requiring substantially lower runtime. Since the optimization model explores the feasible route and parking space exactly, it serves as the reference solution on these smaller instances. In the objective component comparison, the plotted small-scale node size analysis is shown for the 10-, 20-, 30-, and 40-node cases, while the 50-node case is discussed separately in the runtime analysis because the optimization run did not complete within practical memory and time limits.

\subsubsection{Summary}
Overall, the results show that the objective value responds differently depending on which system parameter is changed. Increasing edge density slightly improves the objective value because the additional road connections reduce driving time, while walking time and parking cost remain unchanged. Increasing the maximum walking distance also reduces the objective value overall, mainly because a wider feasible walking range can allow lower-cost parking locations to be selected. In contrast, increasing the network size generally increases the objective value and creates a larger difference between Optimization and the Heuristic in the larger instances, mainly because the selected routes and parking locations produce different travel-time and cost combinations. In these small-scale cases, increasing the parking budget has almost no effect on the final solution, while changing the parking cost range mainly affects the objective through the selected parking-cost component. Increasing parking supply does not necessarily improve the recommendation, since the Heuristic selects cheaper parking with lower driving time but considerably higher walking time at the larger supply levels. Finally, traffic level has the strongest and clearest effect on travel performance, with the objective increasing consistently as congestion increases because of the rise in driving time, while walking time and parking cost remain unchanged. Overall, these experiments show that driving-related parameters such as network structure and traffic mainly affect the travel-time component, whereas parking-related parameters influence the trade-off between walking time and parking cost. The Heuristic matches Optimization in many tested cases, while noticeable differences appear mainly when larger networks or changes in parking availability alter the selected parking location.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:dplof_conclusion}

This chapter presented a Dynamic Parking Lot Optimization Framework (DPLOF) for real-time parking lot level recommendation in urban road networks. The framework formulates parking recommendation as a two-level route and parking optimization problem, in which the driver first travels to a selected parking lot and then walks to the destination. The model jointly considers total travel time, walking distance, parking cost, parking availability, and the user-defined constraints on walking distance and parking cost. The optimization model was used for small-scale validation, which showed that exact solving is useful as a benchmark but becomes computationally expensive as the network size increases. Therefore,
the proposed heuristic was developed for larger instances and compared with
GA, PSO, and ACO. The results depict that the proposed heuristic provides stable and computationally efficient recommendations while remaining competitive with ACO, whereas GA and PSO are more sensitive to route decoding complexity and parameter variations. The SUMO-based fixed-road network instance further shows that DPLOF can be applied to a realistic urban road topology with directed
links. Overall, the findings indicate that DPLOF is suitable for scalable real-time parking lot level recommendations while balancing travel efficiency, walking time, and parking cost. Chapter 5 extends this work by moving the decision from the parking lot to the individual parking spot inside the selected lot.

\chapter{Proposed Dynamic Parking Optimization Framework for Parking
Spot Selection}
\label{ch:dpsof}
\begin{thesisbody}

Urban parking systems require real-time decision-making that reduces parking spot search time, improves space utilization, and provides reliable guidance under changing traffic and occupancy conditions. Existing parking recommendation systems commonly select a parking lot based on travel time, walking distance, cost, and availability. However, these approaches often ignore the internal structure of the parking lot, where individual spots may differ in availability, cost, distance from the entrance, and walking time to the pedestrian exit. To address this limitation, this chapter proposes a Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF) for smart urban parking environments. DPSOF extends parking recommendation from parking lot level selection to parking spot level recommendation by representing each parking spot as a decision option with its own cost, availability, and route characteristics. The framework solves the routing and spot-selection problem dynamically by updating the driver's current node and recomputing the best feasible parking spot as the vehicle progresses node by node through the network. The total cost of selecting a spot considers external driving time, internal parking lot travel time, walking time to the destination, and spot-specific cost, while unavailable spots are excluded before optimization. The problem minimizes a weighted combination of total travel time and parking cost under user-defined walking distance and budget constraints. Meta-heuristic methods, namely Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), are considered for scalable comparison under larger urban scenarios. By selecting a specific parking spot before arrival, the proposed framework reduces post-arrival uncertainty and improves parking space utilization in comparison with conventional lot level recommendation approaches.

\section{Introduction}

The rapid growth of population and vehicle ownership in dense urban areas has made parking spot search a major challenge in modern smart transportation systems. Drivers often spend a considerable amount of time searching for an available parking space near their destination, which increases congestion, travel delay, fuel consumption, emissions, and user frustration \cite{p2-ref1}. Therefore, efficient parking management is required for improving road network performance and for reducing unnecessary vehicle movement in urban areas. Smart Parking Systems (SPS) have been developed to improve parking monitoring, availability detection, reservation, pricing, and guidance by using real-time sensing, communication, and intelligent decision-making methods \cite{p2-ref2}, \cite{p2-ref3}.

\vspace{0.18in}

Existing parking allocation and recommendation systems commonly consider factors such as travel time, walking distance, parking cost, and parking availability. Multi-objective parking allocation methods have been used to balance different parking-related objectives during the recommendation \cite{p2-ref4}, and genetic-algorithm-based approaches have been applied to parking lot selection and allocation performance analysis \cite{p2-ref5}. In addition, data-driven and experience-based optimization methods have been proposed to improve adaptive decision-making in smart parking environments \cite{p2-ref6}. Other studies have considered dynamic parking guidance using ant colony optimization \cite{p2-ref7}, memetic optimization for street parking allocation \cite{p2-ref8}, shared parking reservation and allocation \cite{p2-ref9}, and parking allocation under peak demand conditions \cite{p2-ref10}. These studies show that intelligent optimization can improve parking recommendation when traffic conditions, parking demand, and parking availability vary over time.

\vspace{0.18in}

Recent SPS research has further improved parking management through sensing, automation, reservation, dynamic pricing, and user-centered allocation. Reservation-based valet parking studies show that reservation can improve reliability, but internal movement inside the parking facility is still not fully considered \cite{p2-ref11}. Existing surveys on smart-city parking solutions show that most systems focus on availability detection, pricing, reservation, and facility-level recommendation rather than on a detailed parking spot level path \cite{p2-ref12}. Similarly, IoT-based parking surveys focus on real-time sensing and parking management, but internal movement inside a parking lot is usually not treated as a core optimization component \cite{p2-ref13}. Other studies have highlighted the importance of efficient space utilization \cite{p2-ref14}, dynamic fee design with rematching strategies \cite{p2-ref16}, dynamic pricing and allocation under changing demand \cite{p2-ref17}, and adaptive pricing with virtual voting for parking-slot allocation \cite{p2-ref18}.

\vspace{0.18in}

Although these studies improve parking recommendation and allocation, many existing frameworks still operate mainly at the parking lot level. In these approaches, a parking lot is usually treated as a single candidate location, and the individual parking spots inside the lot are not represented explicitly. As a result, after entering a recommended facility, the driver may still need to search for a suitable available parking space. Competition-based parking reservation systems show that reservation services can reduce uncertainty, but spot level recommendation still requires detailed modeling of the individual parking spaces \cite{p2-ref19}. IoT and machine-learning-based parking systems improve detection and prediction, but they often focus on whether a space is available rather than on how the internal driving and walking inside the facility affect the quality of the recommendation \cite{p2-ref20}. Optimization models that consider fuel, time, and cost further show the importance of evaluating several parking factors together \cite{p2-ref21}, while real-time space detection and path-finding systems motivate the need to connect parking availability with route planning \cite{p2-ref22}.

\vspace{0.18in}

Another important limitation is that many parking recommendation systems solve the route and parking decision only once, usually from the driver's initial location. However, in a real urban road network, the traffic condition and the parking availability may change while the driver is traveling. A parking spot that is suitable at the beginning of the route may become less suitable later because of congestion, occupancy changes, or route updates. Therefore, an effective parking recommendation framework should update the driver's current node, recompute the route, and re-evaluate the feasible parking spots as the vehicle progresses through the network. This node-by-node recomputation allows the parking decision to adapt to the current traffic and parking state instead of relying only on the initial recommendation.

\vspace{0.18in}

To address this gap, this chapter proposes a Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF) for smart urban parking environments. Unlike existing parking lot level recommendation methods, DPSOF extends the parking decision from parking lot level selection to individual parking spot level recommendation. Each parking spot is modeled as a separate graph node with its own cost, availability, and route characteristics. Artificial-intelligence-based parking guidance studies show the importance of intelligent parking decision support \cite{p2-ref23}, and parking management systems that consider occupancy prediction and customer preferences further support the need for more detailed user-aware models \cite{p2-ref24}. Automated real-time parking management systems also show the importance of timely parking space information for practical SPS deployment \cite{p2-ref25}.

\vspace{0.18in}

In the proposed framework, the complete path from the driver's current location to the final destination is represented using both road network links and parking facility links. For each candidate parking spot, the total cost of selecting that spot includes the driving time to the parking lot entrance, the internal driving time to the spot, the walking time from the spot to the pedestrian exit, the walking time from the exit to the destination, and the spot-specific parking cost. Online parking allocation, dynamic parking modeling, and parking space-view-based reservation studies support the importance of real-time decision-making, optimization, and individual parking space consideration in changing parking environments \cite{p2-ref26}, \cite{p2-ref27}, \cite{p2-ref28}. In the proposed model, the input graph is extended with parking spot nodes and internal route links without changing the underlying optimization structure. Unavailable spots are removed during input generation, so only the currently available spots are evaluated as the traffic and occupancy conditions change. This framework supports downtown parking applications by guiding the driver to both a suitable parking lot and a specific available spot inside it. To evaluate scalability, the optimization model and the meta-heuristic approaches are considered under varying traffic, cost, and occupancy conditions, supported by comparative studies of GA and PSO in intelligent parking systems \cite{p2-ref29}. The proposed framework is designed for real-world parking applications, where the driver requires guidance not only to a parking lot but also to the most convenient available parking spot inside that lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

The main contributions of this chapter are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF) is proposed by extending parking recommendation from parking lot level selection to individual parking spot level recommendation.

    \item Each parking spot is modeled as a separate graph node with its own parking identifier, spot-specific cost, availability status, and traversal characteristics.

    \item Internal parking facility traversal is incorporated by adding weighted links from parking lot entrances to parking spots and from parking spots to pedestrian exits.

    \item The total cost of selecting a parking spot considers external driving time, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and spot-specific parking cost.

    \item Dynamic parking availability is handled at the input-generation stage by considering only the currently available parking spots at each optimization step.

    \item The optimization model and the meta-heuristic approaches are considered for scalable comparison under varying traffic, cost, and parking occupancy conditions.

    \item The performance evaluation is conducted using both synthetic urban road network environments and a SUMO-based real-road network case study, in order to assess the proposed framework under controlled and realistic traffic conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:dpsof_related_work}
Although Chapter~\ref{ch:related_work} reviewed the prior literature in detail, this subsection focuses specifically on studies related to the parking spot recommendation problem. Existing research suggests that parking spot recommendation is affected by local availability, the number of spots inside a lot, walking flexibility, congestion, and pricing. This review therefore highlights the main factors and methodological trends in the literature that define the parking spot recommendation problem studied in this chapter.

\vspace{0.18in}


SPS have emerged as an effective solution for reducing the time drivers spend searching for parking in urban areas. Traditional parking systems provide limited parking information and often require the driver to search manually for an available parking space, which increases congestion, fuel consumption, emissions, travel delay, and inefficient use of limited parking facilities. Aljohani et al.~\cite{p2-ref1} reviewed parking solutions for smart cities and discussed sensing, parking guidance, reservation, pricing, and parking management methods. Alam et al.~\cite{p2-ref2} surveyed IoT-driven smart parking management systems and highlighted sensing technologies, system limitations, and future research directions. Agrawal et al.~\cite{p2-ref3} reviewed smart parking techniques and innovations for maximizing urban space, while Almalki and Ragab~\cite{p2-ref4} discussed smart parking challenges and future perspectives in sustainable environments. These survey studies show that SPS have progressed through sensing, communication, automation, and intelligent parking-management technologies. However, many systems still focus on facility-level recommendation, availability detection, or general parking management rather than on detailed parking spot level recommendation.

\vspace{0.18in}

Several studies have focused on real-time parking allocation and optimization. Abdelmagid et al.~\cite{p2-ref5} proposed a dynamic modeling framework for real-time parking allocation. Kulkarni et al.~\cite{p2-ref6} developed a smart automated and real-time parking management system. Amidu et al.~\cite{p2-ref7} presented a primal-dual algorithm for online parking allocation problems. Zheng et al.~\cite{p2-ref8} studied parking space allocation under peak demand in intelligent parking lots. Lian~\cite{p2-ref9} proposed a shared parking spot reservation and allocation model that prioritizes commuting demand. Hu et al.~\cite{p2-ref10} developed a multi-objective intelligent optimization algorithm for parking space allocation. These works show that allocation, reservation, and real-time decision-making are important for improving parking space utilization. However, they generally do not represent the internal route from the parking facility entrance to the selected spot and from that spot to a pedestrian exit.

\vspace{0.18in}

Pricing, reservation, and demand-responsive parking strategies have also been widely studied. Nakazato et al.~\cite{p2-ref11} investigated parking lot allocation using rematching and dynamic parking-fee design. Wang et al.~\cite{p2-ref12} proposed PRPSV to improve parking efficiency and reservation service based on a parking space view. Saharan et al.~\cite{p2-ref13} proposed DyPARK, a dynamic pricing and allocation scheme for smart on-street parking systems. These studies show that reservation, rematching, and pricing can improve parking utilization and reduce uncertainty. However, most of these approaches still focus on facility-level or space-view-based management rather than on complete spot level routing with internal vehicle movement and walking components.

\vspace{0.18in}

Meta-heuristic and search-based methods have also been applied to smart parking problems. Cheng and Nian~\cite{p2-ref14} studied parking lot optimization using a genetic-algorithm-based approach. Cheng and Kang~\cite{p2-ref15} applied a genetic algorithm to path planning for automatic navigation to parking spaces inside parking lots. Abo-Zahhad and Abo-Zahhad~\cite{p2-ref16} proposed an IoT and machine-learning-based smart parking system. Dimlo et al.~\cite{p2-ref17} introduced a GA-RBF-based parking management model to optimize fuel, time, and cost. Dong et al.~\cite{p2-ref18} developed a chaotic particle swarm optimization method for parking space guidance. Wang et al.~\cite{p2-ref19} applied parallel PSO for parking guidance and showed that swarm-based search can support route selection in complex parking environments. Adeyiga et al.~\cite{p2-ref20} compared genetic algorithm and particle swarm optimization techniques in intelligent parking systems. These studies support the use of GA, PSO, and learning-based methods for parking optimization. However, their main focus is on parking guidance, path planning, fuel-time-cost optimization, or general parking space recommendation, rather than on treating each currently available spot as a graph node with its own internal travel and walking characteristics.

\vspace{0.18in}

Other adaptive and intelligent parking methods further show the importance of real-time and scalable decision-making. Xie et al.~\cite{p2-ref21} proposed a self-optimization algorithm for multi-style smart parking based on experience, knowledge, and data. Hassoune et al.~\cite{p2-ref22} developed a dynamic parking guidance architecture using ant colony optimization and multi-agent systems. Bhimavarapu et al.~\cite{p2-ref23} presented an end-to-end system for real-time space detection and path finding. Akandwanaho and Govender~\cite{p2-ref24} applied a memetic algorithm for dynamic street parking space optimization. Baikani et al.~\cite{p2-ref25} proposed NeuroPark Guide as an artificial-intelligence-based parking guidance solution. He et al.~\cite{p2-ref26} developed a smart parking management system that considers occupancy-rate prediction and customer preferences. These studies show the value of adaptive optimization, ACO, real-time detection, path finding, intelligent guidance, and user-aware parking management. However, they mostly focus on detection, prediction, route guidance, or general allocation rather than on detailed spot level recommendation using internal parking facility traversal links.

\vspace{0.18in}

Recent studies have also examined adaptive pricing, autonomous valet parking, and reservation-based services. Hassija et al.~\cite{p2-ref27} proposed a parking-slot allocation framework using virtual voting and adaptive pricing. Zhang et al.~\cite{p2-ref28} studied reservation-enhanced autonomous valet parking under practical constraints. Hu et al.~\cite{p2-ref29} proposed CRPark, a smart parking system with competition and reservation services. These studies show that adaptive pricing, reservation, competition, and valet-parking services can reduce uncertainty and improve parking space allocation. However, spot level recommendation still requires explicit modeling of each available parking spot, its cost, its internal vehicle travel time, its internal walking time, and its connection to the final destination.

\vspace{0.18in}

Overall, existing research has improved SPS through sensing, prediction, automation, allocation, pricing, reservation, path planning, and meta-heuristic optimization. However, most studies still focus on parking lot recommendation, parking space detection, pricing, reservation, or general allocation. Limited attention has been given to frameworks that represent the currently available parking spots as part of the routing network, with separate spot nodes, internal vehicle links, pedestrian-exit links, and spot-specific costs. Motivated by this gap, DPSOF extends parking recommendation from parking lot level selection to parking spot level recommendation. The proposed framework preserves the core route and parking optimization structure while extending the environment graph with available parking spots and internal parking facility traversal links. This allows the optimization model to evaluate the full cost of selecting a parking spot, including external driving time, internal vehicle travel time, internal walking time, external walking time, and the spot-specific parking cost.

\section{System Model and Problem Description}
\label{sec:dpsof_system_model}

This section describes the system model of the proposed DPSOF. The framework consists of a driver, a centralized recommendation server, real-time traffic information, parking spot information, parking cost information, and an urban road network with parking facility traversal links. These components work together to select a suitable parking spot under changing traffic, parking-availability, and parking cost conditions. In SPS, parking prices may change with demand, so the cost of a parking lot and the cost of an individual parking spot can vary over time. Unlike conventional parking recommendation systems that select only a parking lot, DPSOF recommends a specific parking spot inside a parking facility by considering both external road network travel and internal parking facility movement.

\vspace{0.18in}

The urban environment is modeled as a directed graph \(G=(N,E)\), where \(N\) denotes the set of graph nodes and \(E\) denotes the set of directed edges. The nodes represent road intersections, the driver's current node \(A\), the destination node \(D\), the parking facility entrance nodes, the vehicle exit nodes, the pedestrian exit nodes, and the individual parking spot nodes. Each directed edge \((i,j)\in E\) is associated with a traversal weight that represents either vehicle travel time or pedestrian walking time. Therefore, the graph includes both external road network links and internal parking facility links. The road network structure and the parking facility layout remain fixed during the recommendation interval, while the traffic-dependent travel times and the , the parking spot availability and/or cost may change over time.

\vspace{0.18in}

Let \(P=\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_m\}\) denote the set of \(m\) parking facilities, where \(p_i\in P\) represents parking facility \(i\). The driver's current vehicle location is denoted by \(A\). At the initial decision step, \(A\) corresponds to the driver's source node. As the vehicle moves through the road network, \(A\) is updated to the driver's current node, and the routing and parking spot decision is recomputed from that node.

\vspace{0.18in}

Each parking facility \(p_i\) contains a set of currently available parking spots,
\[
S_i=\{s_{i1},s_{i2},\ldots,s_{ik_i}\},
\]
where \(k_i\) denotes the number of currently available candidate spots in facility \(p_i\) at decision time \(t\). Since parking availability changes over time, unavailable or occupied spots are not included in the candidate input set. Instead, the centralized recommendation server retrieves the current parking status and provides only the currently available parking spots to the optimization model.

\vspace{0.18in}

DPSOF follows a spot level route and parking decision process. For each available parking spot \(s_{ij}\), the system evaluates a complete path from the driver's current location to the final destination. At the first level, the driver travels from the current vehicle location \(A\) to the entrance of parking facility \(p_i\). At the second level, the vehicle moves inside the facility from the entrance to the selected parking spot \(s_{ij}\). At the third level, after parking, the driver walks from the selected spot to the pedestrian exit of the facility. At the final level, the driver walks from the pedestrian exit to the destination \(D\).

\vspace{0.18in}

Let \(T^{road}_{i}\) denote the estimated driving time from the current vehicle location \(A\) to the entrance of parking facility \(p_i\) under the current traffic condition. The internal vehicle travel time from the facility entrance to parking spot \(s_{ij}\) is denoted by \(T^{veh}_{ij}\). The internal walking time from parking spot \(s_{ij}\) to the pedestrian exit is denoted by \(T^{walk,in}_{ij}\), and the walking time from the pedestrian exit of facility \(p_i\) to the destination \(D\) is denoted by \(T^{walk,out}_{i}\). Each parking spot also has a spot-specific parking cost \(C^s_{ij}\), which may vary across the spots within the same facility.

\vspace{0.18in}

The total travel time component associated with selecting parking spot \(s_{ij}\) is therefore expressed as

\begin{equation}
T_{ij}(t)=
T^{road}_{i}
+
T^{veh}_{ij}
+
T^{walk,in}_{ij}
+
T^{walk,out}_{i}.
\label{eq:total_time_spot}
\end{equation}

This formulation allows the system to distinguish between different parking spots inside the same parking facility. For example, two spots in the same lot may have the same external driving time from the current vehicle location to the facility entrance, but they may have different internal vehicle travel times, internal walking times, and parking costs, depending on their location inside the facility.

As the vehicle moves through the road network, the centralized recommendation server updates the traffic state and reconstructs the set of available parking spots. When the vehicle reaches a new decision point, the system retrieves the updated road and parking information, evaluates the candidate parking spots using the objective function, and updates the recommended route and the recommended spot. This repeated recomputation continues until the vehicle reaches the selected parking spot. The model uses current traffic and parking-availability information only and does not forecast future traffic conditions or future parking availability.

\vspace{0.18in}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dpsof_system_model} illustrates the proposed system model as an example with two parking lots. Panel (A) shows the external road network, where the driver travels from the source toward the candidate parking facilities \(P1\) and \(P2\). The centralized server receives the updated road network and parking information and evaluates the feasible parking alternatives. Each parking facility has a vehicle entrance, a vehicle exit, parking cost information, and a pedestrian connection from the facility exit to the destination. Panel (B) shows the internal structure of parking facility \(P1\), where the selected spot is represented inside the lot. The internal vehicle path from the entrance to the selected spot, the internal pedestrian path from the selected spot to the pedestrian exit, and the external walking path from the pedestrian exit to the destination are all represented explicitly.

\vspace{0.18in}

    \caption{System model of the Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework with (A) candidate parking facilities and (B) internal structure of \(P1\).}
    \label{fig:dpsof_system_model}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\vspace{0.18in}

Each available parking spot \(s_{ij}\) is characterized by its internal vehicle travel time \(T^{veh}_{ij}\), its internal walking time \(T^{walk,in}_{ij}\), its external walking time \(T^{walk,out}_{i}\), and its spot-specific parking cost \(C^s_{ij}\). A parking spot is excluded from consideration if it is unavailable or occupied at decision time \(t\). In addition, a candidate spot is considered feasible only if it satisfies the user-defined walking and parking cost constraints. The walking constraint is applied to the external walking segment from the pedestrian exit of the facility to the destination, while the internal walking time from the selected spot to the pedestrian exit is included in the objective calculation. This distinction allows the proposed model to capture the walking movement inside the parking facility, which is usually ignored in parking lot level recommendation models.

At each decision step, the system evaluates the tuple
\[
\left(
T^{road}_{i},
T^{veh}_{ij},
T^{walk,in}_{ij},
T^{walk,out}_{i},
C^s_{ij}
\right)
\]
for every available parking spot \(s_{ij}\in S_i\) and every parking facility \(p_i\in P\). The parking spots that satisfy the walking and parking cost constraints are retained as feasible options. These feasible spots are then evaluated by the optimization model and the meta-heuristic baselines under the same system model. Once a spot is selected and confirmed, the centralized server reserves that spot for a predefined reservation threshold \(\tau\). During this interval, the selected spot is blocked from being recommended to any other vehicle. If the driver arrives within \(\tau\), the spot status changes to occupied. Otherwise, the reservation expires and the spot is returned to the available candidate set.

\vspace{0.18in}

Several assumptions are made in the proposed system model. The urban road network structure, the parking facility layout, and the internal traversal links remain fixed during the recommendation interval, while the road-edge travel times may change according to the current traffic condition. The parking spot availability is updated at each decision step, and only the currently available spots are included in the optimization input. The model assumes that the centralized server can retrieve current traffic, parking cost, and parking-availability information. The framework uses current system information only, and future traffic conditions and future parking availability are not forecasted. In addition, the proposed framework extends the parking lot level recommendation model by adding parking spot nodes and internal weighted links while preserving the overall route and parking optimization structure. \section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:dpsof_formulation}

This section formulates the dynamic parking spot recommendation problem based on the system model presented in the previous section. The objective is to select one feasible parking spot for the driver by jointly considering the external road network travel time, the internal vehicle traversal time inside the parking facility, the internal walking time from the selected spot to the pedestrian exit, the external walking time from the facility exit to the destination, the spot-specific parking cost, and the user-defined constraints. At the initial decision step, the current vehicle location \(A\) corresponds to the driver's source node. During route progression, the same formulation is re-evaluated by updating \(A\) to the driver's current node whenever the vehicle reaches a new decision point. In this structure, the destination is reached after the driver first travels to a selected parking facility, parks at the selected spot, and then walks from that spot to the destination through the pedestrian exit.

\subsection{Network Representation and Decision Variables}

Based on the network representation described in the system model, the parking environment is extended from parking lot level candidates to parking spot level candidates. The same route and parking structure used in the parking lot level recommendation model is preserved, but the final parking decision is made at the individual parking spot level. Therefore, the graph \(G=(N,E)\) contains both external road network edges and internal parking facility traversal edges, including the links from parking facility entrances to available parking spots and the links from parking spots to pedestrian exits.

\vspace{0.18in}

The selected parking spot is identified using the binary decision variable \(z_{ij}\). For each currently available parking spot \(s_{ij}\), the binary decision variable in Equation~\eqref{eq:decision_variable} is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
z_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if parking spot } s_{ij} \text{ is selected},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:decision_variable}
\end{equation}

Here, \(z_{ij}=1\) means that parking spot \(s_{ij}\) in parking facility \(p_i\) is recommended to the driver. Since unavailable or occupied spots are removed during the input-generation stage, the optimization model evaluates only the currently available parking spot nodes in the updated environment graph.

\subsection{Objective Function}

The objective function in Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective} minimizes a weighted combination of the total travel time and the spot-specific parking cost:

\begin{equation}
\min Z =
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\sum_{j=1}^{k_i}
z_{ij}
\left[
\alpha T_{ij}(t)
+
(1-\alpha)C^s_{ij}
\right],
\label{eq:spot_objective}
\end{equation}

where \(T_{ij}(t)\) denotes the total travel time associated with selecting parking spot \(s_{ij}\), \(C^s_{ij}\) is the spot-specific parking cost at decision time \(t\), and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is the objective weight parameter that controls the trade-off between travel time and parking cost. Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective} therefore balances travel efficiency and parking cost while reflecting the user preferences. A larger value of \(\alpha\) gives more importance to travel time minimization, while a smaller value gives more importance to parking cost reduction.

\subsection{parking spot Selection Constraint}

The model has to select exactly one currently available parking spot for the driver. This requirement is enforced using Equation~\eqref{eq:one_spot_selection}:

\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\sum_{j=1}^{k_i}
z_{ij}
=
1.
\label{eq:one_spot_selection}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:one_spot_selection} ensures that the solution selects one parking spot from the candidate set generated at decision time \(t\). Since unavailable and occupied spots are removed before optimization, the selected spot necessarily belongs to the currently available set.

\subsection{Walking and parking cost Constraints}

To satisfy the user's walking preference, the selected parking spot has to satisfy the maximum acceptable walking threshold. Since this constraint is applied to the external walking segment from the parking facility exit to the destination, it is written as

\begin{equation}
z_{ij}\,T^{walk,out}_{i}
\leq
W_{\max},
\quad
\forall p_i\in P,\ \forall s_{ij}\in S_i.
\label{eq:walking_constraint}
\end{equation}

Here, \(T^{walk,out}_{i}\) denotes the walking time from the pedestrian exit of facility \(p_i\) to the destination \(D\), and \(W_{\max}\) denotes the maximum acceptable walking threshold. Equation~\eqref{eq:walking_constraint} allows a parking spot to be selected only when the external walking segment satisfies the user's walking constraint. The internal walking time from the selected spot to the pedestrian exit is not used as a feasibility threshold in this constraint, but it is included in the total travel time calculation in Equation~\eqref{eq:total_time_spot}.

\vspace{0.18in}

The user's maximum parking cost preference is enforced using Equation~\eqref{eq:cost_constraint}:

\begin{equation}
z_{ij}\,C^s_{ij}
\leq
C_{\max},
\quad
\forall p_i\in P,\ \forall s_{ij}\in S_i.
\label{eq:cost_constraint}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cost_constraint} ensures that the selected parking spot satisfies the maximum allowable parking cost threshold. Since the cost is defined at the spot level, different spots inside the same parking facility may have different parking costs.

\vspace{0.18in}

Finally, the decision variable is restricted to be binary:

\begin{equation}
z_{ij}\in\{0,1\},
\quad
\forall p_i\in P,\ \forall s_{ij}\in S_i.
\label{eq:binary_constraint}
\end{equation}

Together, Equations~\eqref{eq:one_spot_selection} to~\eqref{eq:binary_constraint} ensure that exactly one currently available parking spot is selected and that it satisfies the user-defined walking and parking cost constraints.

\subsection{Dynamic Availability and Reservation Update}

Parking availability is not modeled as a separate decision variable in the optimization formulation. Instead, the centralized server updates the parking occupancy information at each decision step and reconstructs the candidate set by excluding the unavailable or occupied spots. Therefore, the optimization model remains deterministic for the current system state while still supporting changing parking conditions.

\vspace{0.18in}

Once a parking spot is selected, the reservation mechanism blocks that spot for a predefined reservation threshold \(\tau\). If \(z_{ij}=1\), then the state of the selected spot is updated as

\begin{equation}
q_{ij}(t)=\text{reserved}.
\label{eq:reserved_state}
\end{equation}

At the end of the reservation window, the state of the selected spot is updated using Equation~\eqref{eq:reservation_update}:

\begin{equation}
q_{ij}(t+\tau)=
\begin{cases}
\text{occupied}, & \text{if the driver arrives within } \tau,\\
\text{available}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reservation_update}
\end{equation}

This reservation update prevents the same parking spot from being recommended to another driver during the reservation interval. If the driver does not arrive within the threshold duration, the spot is returned to the available candidate set.

\vspace{0.18in}

At each decision step, the centralized server updates the road travel times, the parking costs, and the parking spot states. It then constructs the available parking spot candidate set, computes the complete travel time and cost components for each candidate spot, removes the infeasible spots, solves the optimization model, and reserves the selected parking spot.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Selected Solution}

Under the proposed formulation, a solution consists of a selected parking facility, a selected parking spot inside that facility, internal vehicle movement from the facility entrance to that spot, and walking movement from the spot to the destination through the pedestrian exit. This structure ensures that the destination is reached after the driver parks at a specific available spot rather than after selecting only a general parking facility.

\vspace{0.18in}

The formulation therefore combines travel time evaluation, spot level parking cost control, walking feasibility, dynamic availability filtering, and reservation update within a single optimization model. Since the recommendation can be recomputed repeatedly as the vehicle progresses through the road network, the computational complexity may increase as the number of parking facilities, available parking spots, and road network links increases. For this reason, the optimization model is mainly suitable for small-scale validation, while scalable meta-heuristic approaches are used for medium-scale and large-scale scenarios.
\section{Meta-Heuristic Baselines}
\label{sec:dpsof_baselines}

This section presents the meta-heuristic baselines used to evaluate the proposed DPSOF. Since the optimization formulation has already been defined in the previous section, the purpose of this section is to describe how GA, PSO, and ACO search for a feasible parking spot using the same objective structure and the same constraints. The spot level recommendation problem becomes computationally challenging as the number of parking facilities, available parking spots, road network links, and internal parking facility traversal links increases. Therefore, GA, PSO, and ACO are used as comparative baselines for evaluating scalable spot level recommendation.

\vspace{0.18in}

All baseline methods follow the same route and parking structure. At each decision step, the centralized server updates the road travel times, the parking costs, and the parking spot states. Only the currently available parking spots are included in the candidate set. A candidate solution represents the selection of one available parking spot \(s_{ij}\in S_i\), together with its complete travel time and cost components. Candidate spots that violate the walking threshold \(W_{\max}\) or the parking cost threshold \(C_{\max}\) are excluded before evaluation. The remaining feasible candidates are evaluated using the same weighted objective in Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective} and the same travel time definition in Equation~\eqref{eq:total_time_spot}. Therefore, GA, PSO, and ACO are compared under the same cost structure, feasibility constraints, and dynamic candidate set as the optimization model.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm}

GA is implemented as an evolutionary baseline. Each chromosome represents one candidate solution and encodes the selected parking facility together with the selected available parking spot inside that facility. During each generation, the chromosomes are evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}. Chromosomes with lower objective values are preferred, because they represent parking options with a lower combined travel time and parking cost.

\vspace{0.18in}

The initial population is generated from the available parking spot candidate set. Selection is applied to favor the better candidates, while crossover and mutation are used to explore alternative parking facilities and parking spots. Elitism is used to preserve the best solution found so far. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpsof_ga_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Genetic Algorithm Baseline}
\label{alg:dpsof_ga_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Parking facilities \(P\), available parking spots \(S_i\), travel time components, spot-specific parking costs, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), population size \(N_{\mathrm{pop}}\), generations \(G_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Selected parking spot \(s^*\)
\STATE Generate an initial population from the currently available parking spots
\STATE \(s^*\leftarrow \emptyset,\; Z^*\leftarrow \infty\)
\FOR{\(g \leftarrow 1\) to \(G_{\max}\)}
    \FOR{each chromosome representing a candidate spot \(s_{ij}\)}
        \STATE Evaluate the total travel time using Equation~\eqref{eq:total_time_spot}
        \STATE Evaluate the objective value using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}
        \IF{\(s_{ij}\) satisfies \(W_{\max}\) and \(C_{\max}\), and the objective value is less than \(Z^*\)}
            \STATE \(s^*\leftarrow s_{ij}\)
            \STATE Update \(Z^*\) with the current objective value
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Apply selection, crossover, and mutation to generate the next population
    \STATE Preserve the best solution using elitism
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \(s^*\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimization}

PSO is implemented as a swarm-based baseline. Each particle represents one candidate solution. The particle position maps to an available parking spot, while the particle velocity guides the movement of the search across the candidate parking spot space. At each iteration, every particle is evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}.

\vspace{0.18in}

Each particle maintains its personal-best solution, while the swarm maintains a global-best solution. After evaluation, the particle positions and velocities are updated using the personal-best and global-best information. The final global-best particle is returned as the PSO-based parking spot. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpsof_pso_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Particle Swarm Optimization Baseline}
\label{alg:dpsof_pso_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Parking facilities \(P\), available parking spots \(S_i\), travel time components, spot-specific parking costs, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), swarm size \(N_{\mathrm{swarm}}\), iterations \(I_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Selected parking spot \(s^*\)
\STATE Initialize particle positions using the currently available parking spots
\STATE Initialize particle velocities
\STATE \(s^*\leftarrow \emptyset,\; Z^*\leftarrow \infty\)
\FOR{\(iter \leftarrow 1\) to \(I_{\max}\)}
    \FOR{each particle}
        \STATE Map the particle position to a candidate parking spot \(s_{ij}\)
        \STATE Evaluate the total travel time using Equation~\eqref{eq:total_time_spot}
        \STATE Evaluate the objective value using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}
        \STATE Update the particle's personal best if the current solution improves it
        \IF{\(s_{ij}\) satisfies \(W_{\max}\) and \(C_{\max}\), and the objective value is less than \(Z^*\)}
            \STATE \(s^*\leftarrow s_{ij}\)
            \STATE Update \(Z^*\) with the current objective value
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Update particle velocities and positions using the personal-best and global-best solutions
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \(s^*\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Ant Colony Optimization}

ACO is implemented as a pheromone-based baseline. Each ant constructs a candidate solution by selecting one available parking spot from the feasible candidate set. The probability of selecting a candidate spot depends on the pheromone intensity and the heuristic desirability. In this work, the heuristic desirability is based on the inverse of the objective value, so the spots with a lower combined travel time and parking cost are more likely to be selected.

\vspace{0.18in}

After all ants have constructed their solutions, each selected spot is evaluated using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}. Pheromone evaporation is applied to reduce the effect of weaker previous choices, and pheromone is deposited on the higher-quality parking spots. The best parking spot found across all iterations is returned as the ACO-based solution. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dpsof_aco_baseline}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Ant Colony Optimization Baseline}
\label{alg:dpsof_aco_baseline}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Data:} Parking facilities \(P\), available parking spots \(S_i\), travel time components, spot-specific parking costs, \(W_{\max}\), \(C_{\max}\), \(\alpha\), number of ants \(N_{\mathrm{ants}}\), iterations \(I_{\max}\)
\STATE \textbf{Result:} Selected parking spot \(s^*\)
\STATE Initialize pheromone values for the currently available parking spots
\STATE \(s^*\leftarrow \emptyset,\; Z^*\leftarrow \infty\)
\FOR{\(iter \leftarrow 1\) to \(I_{\max}\)}
    \FOR{each ant}
        \STATE Select a candidate parking spot \(s_{ij}\) using the pheromone intensity and the heuristic desirability
        \STATE Evaluate the total travel time using Equation~\eqref{eq:total_time_spot}
        \STATE Evaluate the objective value using Equation~\eqref{eq:spot_objective}
        \IF{\(s_{ij}\) satisfies \(W_{\max}\) and \(C_{\max}\), and the objective value is less than \(Z^*\)}
            \STATE \(s^*\leftarrow s_{ij}\)
            \STATE Update \(Z^*\) with the current objective value
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Apply pheromone evaporation
    \STATE Deposit pheromone on the high-quality parking spots
\ENDFOR
\STATE \RETURN \(s^*\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Time Complexity Analysis}

The time complexity of the optimization model and the meta-heuristic baselines is expressed in terms of the number of network nodes \(|V|\), the number of network edges \(|E|\), and the number of candidate parking spots \(|S|\).

\vspace{0.18in}

For the meta-heuristic baselines, the dominant cost comes from repeatedly constructing and evaluating feasible route and spot solutions on the network. If the cost of constructing and evaluating one route and spot solution is approximated as \(O(|V|+|E|+|S|)\), then the complexity of each method can be written directly in terms of \(|V|\), \(|E|\), and \(|S|\).

\vspace{0.18in}

For the GA baseline, let \(N_{pop}\) denote the population size and \(G_{max}\) the number of generations. Since all individuals are evaluated in every generation, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(G_{max}N_{pop}(|V|+|E|+|S|)),
\]
in addition to lower-order costs for selection, crossover, and mutation.

\vspace{0.18in}

For the PSO baseline, let \(N_{swarm}\) denote the swarm size and \(I_{max}\) the number of iterations. Since every particle must be evaluated at every iteration, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(I_{max}N_{swarm}(|V|+|E|+|S|)),
\]
together with lower-order costs for velocity and position updates.

\vspace{0.18in}

For the ACO baseline, let \(N_{ants}\) denote the number of ants. In each iteration, every ant constructs and evaluates a route and spot solution, and the pheromone values on the network edges are then updated. Hence, the dominant complexity is
\[
O(I_{max}(N_{ants}(|V|+|E|+|S|)+|E|)).
\]

This analysis shows that the meta-heuristic baselines are computationally more scalable than the optimization model, because they avoid exhaustive enumeration of the spot-level decision space. This also helps explain why the optimization model becomes impractical beyond the smallest cases, while GA, PSO, and ACO remain computationally feasible in the experiments.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:dpsof_results}

\subsection{Simulation Setup, Performance Metrics, and Parameters}

The experimental evaluation is conducted on controlled synthetic urban-like road networks generated at multiple scales, together with a SUMO-based real-road network for fixed network validation. In this framework, the parking recommendation problem is modeled at the parking spot level rather than only at the parking lot level. Each parking lot is expanded into multiple individual parking spots, and the recommendation process accounts for spot-specific availability, internal access time, internal walking time, and parking cost. This formulation is intended to reflect realistic parking behavior more closely, since the driver is not only choosing a parking facility but is effectively looking for a feasible and convenient parking spot inside that facility.

\vspace{0.18in}

The evaluation follows the multi-level route and parking structure described in the system model. The driver first travels from the source node to the entrance of a selected parking lot, then moves internally to the selected spot, and finally walks from that spot to the destination through the pedestrian exit. In contrast to the lot level formulation, this framework models the differences among spots inside the same lot explicitly. Therefore, even when two candidate spots belong to the same parking lot, they may differ in availability, internal driving effort, internal walking effort, and final parking charge.

\vspace{0.18in}

Datasets are divided into small-, medium-, and large-scale instances. The small-scale instances contain 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 nodes and are used to compare the optimization model with the meta-heuristic baselines. The medium-scale and large-scale scalability analyses use 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, and 2500 nodes. The remaining parameter sensitivity experiments are conducted on the fixed 2500-node instance. The SUMO-based evaluation uses a fixed 2055-node real-road network for realistic fixed network validation.

\vspace{0.18in}

The baseline road network setting uses a directed urban road graph, a parking lot supply of approximately \(5\%\) of the nodes, a maximum walking distance \(W_{\max}=0.50\,\mathrm{km}\), an objective weight \(\alpha=0.90\), a parking cost cap \(C_{\max}=20.5\), and a parking cost range from \(8.0\) to \(20.5\). Low traffic driving speeds are sampled from \(50\) to \(70\,\mathrm{km/h}\), and the walking speed is fixed at \(v_{\mathrm{walk}}=5.0\,\mathrm{km/h}\). On top of this baseline, a spot level parking layer is generated for every parking lot.

\vspace{0.18in}

In the baseline setting, each parking lot is assigned a variable number of parking spots. Rather than using a fixed count for all lots, the number of spots per lot is generated within the range of 20 to 40 spots. Spot availability is modeled explicitly, and approximately \(10\%\) of the generated spots are marked as available in the default setting, while each parking lot is guaranteed to contain at least one available spot. This avoids degenerate cases in which a parking lot is included in the candidate set but contains no feasible spot.

\vspace{0.18in}

To represent realistic internal parking behavior, the generated spots are divided into three classes, namely near, mid, and far. Near spots have a relatively low internal driving time and a low internal walking time, mid spots have moderate internal access characteristics, and far spots have higher internal access times. The spot costs are derived from the parent parking lot cost, so that near spots are generally more expensive, mid spots remaining close to the parent lot cost, and far spots are generally cheaper. In addition, the spot generation process enforces uniqueness in the attribute combinations inside each parking lot, so that no two spots are identical across all the key generated values.

\vspace{0.18in}

The evaluation follows a controlled parameter variation setting, which is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:spot_ps_settings}. For each parameter sensitivity experiment, one parameter is varied while the remaining settings are kept at their baseline values. The walking distance analysis uses \(W_{\max}=0.25\), \(0.50\), \(0.75\), and \(1.00\,\mathrm{km}\). The parking spot availability analysis uses approximately \(10\%\), \(20\%\), \(30\%\), and \(40\%\) available parking spots. The spots per lot analysis uses variable spot count ranges of 10--20, 20--40, 40--60, and 60--80 spots per parking lot. The parking cost variation tests the ranges \(8.0\)--\(20.5\), \(10.0\)--\(22.5\), \(12.5\)--\(25.0\), and \(15.0\)--\(30.0\). The traffic level analysis considers very low, low, medium, and high traffic using speed ranges of \(60\)--\(80\), \(50\)--\(70\), \(30\)--\(49\), and \(10\)--\(29\,\mathrm{km/h}\), respectively. For the edge density variation, the x-axis reports the actual number of directed edges in each scenario.

The main performance metrics are driving time, walking time, parking cost, objective value, and runtime. Driving time represents the travel time from the source node to the selected parking lot, while walking time shows the time required to walk from the selected lot to the destination. Together, these components form the total travel time. Parking cost depicts the parking cost associated with the selected parking option, and runtime measures the computational efficiency of each method.
For every scenario, all methods are evaluated under the same experimental conditions, including the similar road network, source and destination nodes, parking candidates, maximum walking distance constraint, parking cost limit, and objective weight parameter. This ensures that the comparison reflects differences between the methods rather than differences in the underlying test instance.

\subsection{Small-Scale Evaluation}

The small-scale experiments compare the optimization model with the ACO, PSO, and GA baselines on synthetic spot level instances. These experiments are used to assess whether the meta-heuristic methods can approach the optimization benchmark while avoiding the rapid growth in computational cost that appears when exact spot level enumeration is applied to larger instances. In the plotted objective component comparison, the node size analysis is reported for the 10-, 20-, 30-, and 40-node cases.

\vspace{0.18in}

At the spot level, the optimization model becomes expensive more quickly than in the lot level framework because the decision space expands from parking lot selection to individual parking spot selection. The runtime plot therefore plays an important role in the small-scale analysis. The optimization runtime is \(0.56\,\mathrm{s}\) at 10 nodes and \(0.30\,\mathrm{s}\) at 20 nodes, but it rises sharply to \(1382.14\,\mathrm{s}\) at 30 nodes and \(189890.48\,\mathrm{s}\) at 40 nodes. In contrast, ACO remains below \(0.10\,\mathrm{s}\) over the plotted range, PSO is under \(0.25\,\mathrm{s}\), and GA is less than \(0.18\,\mathrm{s}\). These values confirms that exact spot level optimization is useful as a benchmark for small instances, but the meta-heuristic methods are much more practical once the number of feasible parking spots grows.

\vspace{0.18in}

Fig.~\ref{fig:spot_small_scale_runtime} compares the runtime of the optimization model and the meta-heuristic methods. As the network size increases, the computational cost of optimization grows sharply, while the meta-heuristic methods remain computationally inexpensive. The 50-node exact case did not finish within practical limits, whereas the meta-heuristic methods still returned solutions quickly. This result confirms the practical motivation for using meta-heuristic methods instead of optimization beyond the smallest instances.

The SUMO-based evaluation is required because strong performance on synthetic graphs does not guarantee stable behavior on a realistic urban topology with one-way roads, irregular connectivity, and a real-road travel structure. \section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:DPSOF_conclusion}

This chapter presented a Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF) for real-time parking recommendation at the parking spot level. In contrast to the parking lot level framework of Chapter~4, the proposed model extends the decision process from selecting a parking lot to selecting an individual feasible parking spot inside that lot. The framework jointly considers external driving time, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, parking cost, and the user-defined constraints on walking distance and parking budget. In this way, the recommendation reflects a more detailed parking decision process in which the selected spot, and not only the parking facility, affects the overall quality of the solution.


\vspace{0.18in}


The experimental results show that the spot level formulation is able to capture meaningful trade-offs across synthetic urban-like road networks and the SUMO-based real-road network. In the small-scale analysis, the optimization model provides a useful benchmark for validating the formulation, while the meta-heuristic methods produce competitive solutions at a much lower computational cost. As the problem size increases, the optimization approach becomes impractical because the decision space expands significantly with the number of parking spots, whereas ACO, PSO, and GA remain computationally feasible. Among the compared meta-heuristic methods, ACO is generally the most stable and frequently gives the best or near-best objective values across the medium-scale, large-scale, and SUMO-based experiments. PSO and GA also remain competitive, although they are generally more variable across parameter settings.


\vspace{0.18in}


The parameter sensitivity analysis further shows that the spot level recommendation responds in a consistent way to changes in the experimental settings. Greater parking spot availability and a larger number of spots per lot generally improve the objective value by increasing the chance of selecting a better feasible spot. A larger walking distance allowance also tends to improve the objective value by expanding the feasible spot set, although the resulting trade-off depends on the balance between additional walking effort and lower parking cost. Higher parking cost ranges increase the objective value mainly through the parking cost component. Heavier traffic increases the objective value mainly through the driving time component. The parking budget analysis shows that increasing the budget does not necessarily improve the total objective, because the additional feasible spots may be more expensive without producing enough travel time savings to offset their cost.


\vspace{0.18in}


The SUMO-based fixed-road network evaluation confirms that the spot level framework remains effective on a realistic directed urban topology. The results show that the three meta-heuristic methods remain close to one another in many SUMO scenarios, with ACO most often giving the strongest overall performance. This realistic-network evaluation is important because it shows that the framework is not limited to synthetic graph settings and can still produce meaningful parking spot recommendations when the road structure is irregular, directed, and more complex.


\vspace{0.18in}


Overall, the findings of this chapter indicate that DPSOF is a practical extension of the parking lot level recommendation framework. By incorporating internal parking lot movements, spot-specific availability, and spot level cost variation, the framework provides a more detailed and realistic parking recommendation model while remaining computationally manageable through scalable meta-heuristic solution approaches.


\vspace{0.18in}


Several directions remain open for future work. First, the framework can be extended to include time-varying parking spot occupancy and reservation updates, so that a spot may become unavailable while the driver is still traveling toward it. Second, the current framework assumes a single-user recommendation setting; future work could consider multi-vehicle competition for parking spots and shared-capacity effects across multiple drivers. Third, the internal parking lot representation could be further refined using more realistic lot geometries, entrance-exit rules, and pedestrian routing structures. Fourth, the framework could be extended to incorporate electric-vehicle-specific considerations such as charging availability, charging duration, and charger-type preference. Finally, future work may investigate adaptive or learning-based search strategies that combine the strengths of optimization and meta-heuristic methods in order to improve both runtime and solution quality on large-scale dynamic urban networks.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{sec:dpsof_conclusion} 
\begin{thesisbody}

Dynamic parking recommendation has become an increasingly important component of intelligent transportation systems as rapid urbanization and increasing vehicle ownership continue to intensify parking demand in metropolitan areas. Traditional parking guidance systems typically select a parking facility once before the journey begins and assume that traffic conditions, parking availability, and travel costs remain unchanged during the trip. However, these assumptions are rarely valid in real urban environments where congestion, parking occupancy, and travel conditions continuously evolve. Consequently, parking decisions based on static information often lead to unnecessary driving, increased walking distances, higher parking costs, and additional traffic congestion. Motivated by these challenges, this thesis investigated dynamic parking recommendation through two complementary optimization frameworks. The first framework focused on dynamic parking lot recommendation, while the second extended the problem to dynamic parking spot recommendation by incorporating detailed parking space characteristics into the optimization process.

\vspace{0.18in}

The first part of this thesis developed the Dynamic Parking Lot Optimization Framework (DPLOF), which addresses the problem of selecting an appropriate parking lot under continuously changing traffic conditions. The framework models the road network as a weighted graph and repeatedly recomputes parking recommendations whenever updated traffic information becomes available. The objective function jointly minimizes total travel time, consisting of driving and walking time, together with parking cost while satisfying user-defined walking distance and parking budget constraints. To evaluate the framework comprehensively, an optimization model was developed for small-scale instances to provide optimal benchmark solutions, while scalable solution approaches based on a heuristic, Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) were implemented for larger transportation networks where optimization becomes computationally impractical.

\vspace{0.18in}

Extensive computational experiments demonstrated that the proposed parking lot recommendation framework effectively balances travel efficiency and parking cost across a wide range of experimental settings. The optimization model consistently produced optimal solutions for small-scale instances and served as a benchmark for evaluating the heuristic and metaheuristic approaches. For larger synthetic networks and SUMO-based road networks, the heuristic and metaheuristic methods maintained high solution quality while providing significantly lower computational times than optimization. The experimental analysis further showed that traffic conditions, walking distance constraints, parking budget limits, and network size all influence parking recommendations in predictable ways, confirming the robustness and scalability of the proposed framework for dynamic urban parking scenarios.

\vspace{0.18in}

The second part of this thesis extended the parking recommendation problem by introducing the Dynamic Parking Spot Optimization Framework (DPSOF). Rather than selecting only a parking lot, the proposed extension performs optimization directly at the individual parking space level. Each parking space is represented explicitly within the optimization model together with its own availability, parking cost, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and parent parking facility. Consequently, parking recommendation is formulated as a joint parking lot and parking spot optimization problem that captures operational details ignored by conventional parking lot-based approaches.

\vspace{0.18in}

To solve this extended problem, the optimization formulation was redesigned so that the decision variable corresponds to individual parking spaces instead of parking lots. The optimization model was implemented through exhaustive enumeration for small-scale networks to obtain optimal benchmark solutions, while GA, PSO, and ACO were redesigned to search directly over feasible parking space candidates using a unified feasibility evaluation and objective-function calculation. The three metaheuristic algorithms differ only in their search strategies, whereas feasibility checking, objective-value computation, and spot evaluation remain identical across all methods. This unified formulation enables a fair comparison of optimization algorithms while ensuring consistent objective evaluation throughout the experimental study.

\vspace{0.18in}

The computational results demonstrate that incorporating parking space-level information provides a more realistic representation of urban parking than conventional parking lot recommendation alone. The extension successfully models internal vehicle movement within parking facilities, pedestrian movement inside parking lots, individual parking space availability, and spot-specific parking costs while preserving computational scalability. The experimental evaluation showed that the proposed optimization and metaheuristic methods consistently satisfy feasibility constraints while producing high-quality parking recommendations across all tested scenarios. The extension therefore complements the baseline framework by increasing modeling fidelity without fundamentally changing the overall optimization objective.

\vspace{0.18in}

Taken together, the baseline and extension frameworks developed in this thesis provide a comprehensive optimization framework for dynamic parking recommendation. The baseline framework demonstrates that dynamic parking lot recommendation can effectively reduce travel time and parking cost through repeated optimization under changing traffic conditions. The extension further improves decision quality by incorporating individual parking space characteristics into the optimization process, allowing recommendations to account for internal parking facility movements and parking space availability. Collectively, the proposed frameworks illustrate that realistic parking recommendation requires integrating transportation-network conditions with parking facility characteristics rather than treating them as independent decision problems.

\vspace{0.18in}

The research presented in this thesis contributes to intelligent transportation systems in several important ways. First, it demonstrates that repeated traffic-aware parking optimization substantially improves parking decisions compared with static recommendation approaches. Second, it introduces a unified objective function that simultaneously considers travel time and parking cost under user-defined operational constraints. Third, it presents a scalable optimization methodology capable of solving both parking lot and parking space recommendation problems across synthetic and realistic transportation networks. Finally, the thesis establishes optimization benchmarks together with scalable heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms, providing both rigorous evaluation and practical applicability for large-scale deployment.

\vspace{0.18in}

Future research can extend the proposed frameworks in several directions. Reinforcement Learning, Deep Reinforcement Learning, and hybrid optimization approaches combining learning-based methods with evolutionary optimization may further improve real-time parking recommendation performance. The current single-vehicle formulation can be extended to coordinated multi-vehicle parking assignment where multiple drivers compete simultaneously for available parking spaces. Additional realism may be introduced by incorporating predictive parking availability models, real-time parking reservation systems, connected vehicle communication, dynamic parking pricing, electric-vehicle charging infrastructure, autonomous vehicle parking behavior, and stochastic traffic prediction. Furthermore, future work may investigate digital twin environments that continuously synchronize real parking systems with optimization algorithms to support large-scale intelligent city applications.

\vspace{0.18in}

Overall, this thesis advances the state of dynamic parking recommendation by developing optimization frameworks that are both computationally efficient and operationally realistic. The proposed baseline framework establishes an effective methodology for dynamic parking lot recommendation, while the proposed extension further improves realism through parking space-level optimization that explicitly models parking space availability, internal driving time, internal walking time, and external pedestrian movement. The results demonstrate that integrating dynamic traffic information with detailed parking facility modeling provides a practical foundation for next-generation intelligent parking systems capable of supporting efficient, scalable, and real-time urban mobility.

\section{Limitations of the Work}

Although the proposed parking recommendation frameworks provide significant improvements over conventional parking guidance approaches, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both the parking lot and spot-level frameworks consider a single vehicle making an independent parking decision. This assumption does not capture interactions among multiple drivers simultaneously competing for the same parking resources.

\vspace{0.18in}

Second, the proposed models assume that accurate traffic conditions and parking availability information are available when the optimization process is executed. In practical deployments, sensor errors, communication delays, and outdated traffic or occupancy information may affect recommendation quality.

\vspace{0.18in}

Third, the extension represents parking facilities using predefined internal driving and walking times for individual parking spaces. Although this provides greater detail than the parking lot-level model, factors such as internal congestion, driver search behavior, parking maneuvers, pedestrian interactions, and temporary obstructions are not explicitly modeled.

\vspace{0.18in}

Fourth, the exact optimization approach is computationally practical only for relatively small problem instances and becomes increasingly expensive as network size grows. Consequently, larger instances rely on heuristic and metaheuristic methods such as GA, PSO, and ACO, which do not guarantee globally optimal solutions and whose performance may depend on algorithm parameters and computational resources.

\vspace{0.18in}

Finally, the experimental evaluation relies primarily on synthetic transportation networks and SUMO-based simulations. Although these experiments provide controlled evaluation under varying conditions, validation using real-world parking and traffic datasets from multiple urban environments is required to further establish the practical applicability and generalizability of the proposed frameworks.
\end{thesisbody}

\chapter*{Glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Glossary}
\begin{singlespace}
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.45in}{0.45in}

\noindent \textbf{Candidate Parking Lot:}  
A parking lot considered as a feasible recommendation option in the road network. Each candidate lot is evaluated using travel time, walking time, parking cost, and feasibility constraints.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Candidate Parking Spot:}  
An individual parking spot considered as a feasible assignment option inside a parking facility. Each candidate spot is evaluated using availability, internal driving time, internal walking time, external walking time, and parking cost.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Constraint:}  
A limiting condition imposed on the recommendation problem, such as the maximum allowable walking distance or maximum allowable parking cost.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Directed Edge:}  
A road network connection with a specified direction from one node to another. Directed edges represent directional vehicle movement in the transportation graph.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Dominance Filtering:}  
A candidate-reduction step in which a parking option is removed if another feasible option is no yield higher objective values in all relevant criteria and strictly better in at least one criterion.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Dynamic Parking Recommendation:}  
A recommendation process in which the selected parking option can be updated as the driver moves and traffic or parking conditions change.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Edge Density:}  
The level of connectivity in the road network, represented by the number of directed edges among nodes.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{External Driving Time:}  
The driving time from the current vehicle location to the selected parking lot or to the entrance of the selected parking facility.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{External Walking Time:}  
The walking time from the selected parking lot, or from the pedestrian exit of the selected parking facility, to the final destination.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Feasible Parking Option:}  
A parking lot or parking spot that satisfies all imposed constraints, including availability, walking distance threshold, and parking cost threshold.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Graph-Based Representation:}  
A mathematical representation of the transportation and parking system in which nodes represent intersections, parking lots, parking spots, entrances, exits, source, and destination, while edges represent road or walking connections.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Guided Depth-First Search Decoder:}  
A route-construction procedure used by the meta-heuristic comparison methods at the parking lot level to build a valid route from the source to the selected parking lot.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Internal Driving Time:}  
The vehicle travel time from the entrance of a parking facility to a selected parking spot inside that facility.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Internal Walking Time:}  
The walking time from a selected parking spot to the pedestrian exit inside the parking facility.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Meta-Heuristic Method:}  
A search-based optimization method used to obtain near-optimal solutions for computationally complex problems. In this thesis, GA, PSO, and ACO are used as comparative methods.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Objective Function:}  
A mathematical expression that combines total travel time and parking cost into a single value to be minimized.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Objective Weight:}  
The parameter controlling the trade-off between travel time and parking cost in the objective function.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Parking Facility:}  
A parking lot together with its associated parking spots, entrance, and pedestrian exit.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Parking Spot Assignment:}  
The process of selecting an individual parking spot within a parking facility.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Parking Spot Availability:}  
The current occupancy status of a parking spot indicating whether it can be assigned.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{parking cost Threshold:}  
The maximum parking cost acceptable to the user or imposed in the experiment.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Reservation Threshold:}  
The predefined time interval during which an assigned parking spot is reserved for a driver before being released back to the available set.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{road network Scalability:}  
The ability of a solution approach to remain computationally practical as network size and problem complexity increase.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Spot-Specific Parking Cost:}  
The parking cost associated with an individual parking spot, which may differ across spots in the same facility.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{Synthetic Urban-Like Dataset:}  
An artificially generated dataset designed to mimic urban road network and parking characteristics while allowing controlled parameter variation.

\vspace{0.12in}

\noindent \textbf{walking distance Threshold:}  
The maximum allowable walking distance between the selected parking location and the final destination.

\end{adjustwidth}
\end{singlespace}
